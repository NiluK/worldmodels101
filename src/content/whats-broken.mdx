Every chapter so far has ended with something that does not work yet.

This one collects them, because the gap between what these systems can do and
what the announcements imply is where most of the confusion in this subject
lives, and because knowing what is broken is the difference between reading a
paper and being sold one.

## Rollouts do not fail all at once

The usual way to report how long a model holds up is an average error over a
rollout. That number is close to useless, because the properties of a scene
break in an order, and the order is not the one you would guess.

Colour and lighting are stable almost indefinitely. Texture holds up well.
What goes first is whether that is still the same object it was a moment ago,
and whether things are still behaving as though they have mass.

Which is precisely backwards from what you want, and it is not a coincidence.
A loss computed per frame rewards getting most of the pixels approximately
right, and most of the pixels are surfaces and light. Nothing in that loss is
watching whether the chair you walked past is the same chair.

So a rollout can be a hundred steps past the point of being useful and still
look, in any single frame, completely fine. That is the specific way this
subject is easy to fool yourself about.

## The three that keep coming back

**Things stop being themselves.** Object permanence is the one everyone
notices, and it is worth being precise about why it is hard. Nothing in these
systems is storing an object. Consistency is a property of a well-trained
predictor, so it degrades the way predictions degrade, gradually and without
warning. There is no error message when the chair becomes a different chair.

**What if is not really available.** A model can tell you what is likely to
happen next. Asking what would have happened if you had done something else is
a different question, and the answer you get is true inside the model rather
than about the world. Where the model has data the two are close. Where it does
not, they are not, and nothing in the answer indicates which case you are in.

<MarginNote>
This one is easy to under-rate because the model always answers. A system that
said "I have never seen anything like that" would be more useful and less
impressive, and nobody is rewarded for building it.
</MarginNote>

**Long horizons are still the binding constraint.** Every trick in this course
is, read uncharitably, a way of not needing the model to be right for very
long. Short rollouts. Replanning. Starting from real states. They work. They
also amount to an admission that the thing does not hold up, and the horizon
over which it does is the number that decides what you can build.

## Nobody can tell you which one is best

This is the part that should bother you most, and it gets the least attention.

There is no agreed way to measure whether a generated world is behaving. There
are several disagreeing ways, and a system that wins on one routinely loses on
another.

Frame quality is measurable and is what demos are made of. Whether the world
holds together over a thousand steps is what an agent needs, and no demo is
long enough to show it. Whether the action you gave it is the action it took
is the difference between a world model and a video, and it is almost never
reported. Cost decides whether anything can afford to search inside the thing,
and it is reported least of all.

Downstream task success looks like the honest answer, and it has its own
problem: it scores the model and the policy together, so a good policy hides a
bad model and the number tells you nothing about which you have.

None of this means progress is not happening. It means claims about progress
are currently claims about whichever measure the claimant chose.

## What to do with all this

Four questions, which between them handle most of what you will read.

**What does it output?** A picture, a structure, a compact state, an embedding.
This decides what it can be used for and almost nothing else follows without
it.

**Can you roll it forward under actions nobody took?** If not, it is not going
to support a decision, however good it looks.

**Over what horizon does it hold up, and who measured that?** The number
matters more than the architecture, and it is usually missing.

**Which measure is the claim using, and what does that measure ignore?** There
is no overall score. Anyone implying there is has picked one and not told you.

That is the whole toolkit. It is unglamorous, and it will get you further
through a paper than knowing any particular architecture.
