Every one of these systems ends on something that does not work yet. The
failures belong together, because the confusion around the phrase was built out
of them. Five kept turning up, persistence, action, horizon, target and
verification, each found by a different community and measured late or not at
all. Knowing which one a system hits first separates reading a paper from being
sold one.

## Rollouts do not fail all at once

A rollout is the model's predictions run forward step after step. The usual
report, one average error over the run, melts several failures into one number
and hides the order they happened in.

Identity can go while texture stays sharp. Action fidelity, whether the action
you gave it is the action it took, can go while both still look fine. Which
goes first depends on the system and the task, so the useful report is one
horizon per property: how many steps each held up for.

In this profile, colour and texture outlast identity and physics, and that is
no accident. A per-frame loss pays the model for getting most of the pixels
about right, and most pixels are surfaces and light. Nothing in it is watching
whether the chair you walked past is the same chair.

The result is the single-frame alibi: a rollout a hundred steps past the point
of being useful that still looks, in any one frame, completely fine. Every
frame has an alibi and only the sequence is guilty.

## Three failures that outlived every fix

**Things stop being themselves.** Object permanence, the expectation that a
thing is still there, and still itself, when you look back, is the failure
everyone notices. It is the one chapter 1's turn-around test was built to
catch, and could not.

Take a renderer, a model whose output is pictures. It has no object store, so
identity lives in the predictor's hidden state, the numbers it carries between
steps. When the chair becomes another chair, it does so a little at a time and
without an error message. A simulator stores the object explicitly, and pays by
needing the structure right first.

**What if is not really available.** A model can tell you what is likely to
happen next. What would have happened if you had done something else is a
different question, and for a decision it is the only one. The answer is true
inside the model rather than about the world.

Bingyi Kang and colleagues measured the gap in 2024, in *How Far is Video
Generation from World Model*. They tested video generators on physical laws
inside and outside their training data. Inside, the generators obeyed the
laws. Outside, they broke them, and nothing in the output said which case you
were in.

<MarginNote>
This one gets under-rated because the model always answers. A system that said
"I have never seen anything like that" would be more useful and less
impressive, and nobody is rewarded for building it.
</MarginNote>

**Long horizons are still the binding constraint.** Read unkindly, every trick
in model-based control is a way of not needing the model to be right for very long.
Horizon was the first of the five to be measured, by the people who had to act
on the model's word: reinforcement learning.

Tingwu Wang and colleagues did it in 2019, in *Benchmarking Model-Based
Reinforcement Learning*. They ran the methods that learn a model of their
environment and plan inside it side by side. The planning horizon, how many
steps ahead the model was trusted, decided where each won and lost.

Michael Janner and colleagues answered the same year under a title that is
still the question, *When to Trust Your Model*. Only briefly, and from a real
state. Short rollouts and replanning every few steps work, and they are a
confession that the model does not hold up. The horizon over which it does
still decides what you can build.

## The league table nobody can publish

Measurement arrived in the wrong order. Reinforcement learning measured horizon
first because an agent that trusts a bad model loses, and the loss shows up in
the score. The video people came next and measured what they could: the frame.

VBench, a 2023 benchmark from Ziqi Huang and colleagues, scored generated video
on separate dimensions such as frame quality and motion smoothness. It used
enough of them that the ordering stopped being obvious.

Benchmarks that test worlds you act inside rather than watch came last.
PlayWorld, a benchmark released in August 2026, tests 171
scenarios. They cover geometry, interaction fidelity (does an action do what it
should) and what happens to objects as they leave and re-enter view. That is
persistence and action, the two failures a renderer hits first, tested seven
years after horizon.

Frame quality is measurable and is what demos are made of. Whether the world
holds together over a thousand steps is what an agent needs, and no demo is
long enough to show it. Action fidelity is the difference between a world model
and a video, and it is almost never reported. Cost is reported least of all.

That order is close to the reverse of how much each matters, and I do not think
it is by chance. Downstream task success looks like the honest way out, but it
scores the model and the policy together, and a good policy hides a bad model.

Progress is real. Genie 3, from Google DeepMind in 2025, is said to stay
coherent for several minutes, reported by the lab that built it on a measure it
chose. "Better" is an incomplete sentence until the measure is named, and the
system contract with it, meaning which of the five machines is claimed.

## Match the failure test to the system

The phrase covers five machines, and for most of this history they sat the same
exam. That is where the confusion came from. A renderer lives or dies by
persistence and action fidelity, and the single-frame alibi is its native
failure. A simulator is the easy one to check, because it exports structure
another program can test.

A compact dynamics model predicts a small state rather than pixels. It depends
on rollout horizon, and on whether planning search can exploit it. A
representation, an embedding learned for something else to use, can look stable
while throwing away the variable the task needed. Call that the target problem:
predicting the wrong thing well.

An implicit model is a claim that a model formed inside a network trained for
some other job. Checking it needs a probe, a small second network trained to
read one fact out of the first's internals. Kenneth Li and colleagues showed
what that takes in 2022, in *Emergent World Representations*.

A network trained only to predict the next move in Othello (a board game played
with two-sided discs) turned out, under probes, to hold a picture of the board.
The probe could have found nothing, which is what makes it evidence.

Four questions still handle most papers:

**What does it output?** A picture, a structure, a compact state, an embedding.
Nothing else follows without it.

**Can you roll it forward under actions nobody took?** If not, it will not
support a decision, however good it looks.

**Over what horizon does it hold up, and who measured that?** The number
matters more than the architecture, and it is usually missing.

**Which measure is the claim using, and what does it ignore?** There is no
overall score, so anyone implying one has picked a measure and not told you
which.

Yann LeCun (then Meta's chief AI scientist, and a Turing Award winner) wrote the
fullest statement of the target in 2022, *A Path Towards Autonomous Machine
Intelligence*: what a world model would have to do to deserve the name. Read
against the four questions, it is a list of what is still missing. Architecture
names answer none of the four.
