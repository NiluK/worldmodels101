A professional tennis serve leaves the racket at around 200 km/h and arrives
about 500 milliseconds later. In that window the returner has to read the ball,
choose a shot, move two or three metres, and swing.

The problem is that none of those numbers work. Signal from retina to visual
cortex takes something like 60&ndash;100 ms. Getting a large muscle group to
actually move takes another 150 ms or so. By the time the returner *knows*
where the ball is, it has already travelled several metres past there. Add the
time to swing and you are over budget before you have decided anything.

So the returner is not reacting to the ball. They cannot be. They are reacting
to a **prediction** of the ball: a forecast produced by something in their
nervous system that took the server's shoulder rotation, the toss, the
racket angle, and the first few frames of flight, and extrapolated.

<MarginNote>
Cricket batters facing a 145 km/h delivery have roughly 400 ms and the same
problem. Eye-tracking studies find that good batters make a predictive saccade
to where the ball is *going* to bounce, ahead of it arriving, and that
this is one of the things separating elite batters from merely good ones.
</MarginNote>

That forecasting machinery is the subject of this chapter.

## A definition

Everything from here to Chapter 6 uses the Dynamics Model definition.
So: a **world model** is a learned, internal simulator of an environment:
something that, given the current state of the world and an action under
consideration, returns what the world will look like next.

Written down, it is a conditional distribution over next states:

$$
p_\theta(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)
$$

where $s_t$ is the state at time $t$, $a_t$ is the action taken, and $\theta$
are learned parameters. Often you also want a reward model $r_\theta(s_t, a_t)$
if you intend to do anything goal-directed with it.

Each part of that definition hides a problem, and each problem turns into a
chapter later.

**It is learned.** A hand-written physics engine fits the functional form and
nobody calls it a world model. The interesting case is when $p_\theta$ is
fitted from observed experience, which means it can cover domains nobody has
written equations for: cloth, crowds, other agents, the behaviour of a
user interface.

**It is generative in the sense that matters: it can be rolled out.** Apply it
to its own output and you get $s_{t+2}$, then $s_{t+3}$. A single-step
predictor you can iterate becomes a simulator you can search inside. This is
where the power comes from and, as Chapter 5 shows, where it breaks down.

**You almost never have $s_t$.** You have pixels, or audio, or a sensor
reading. The true state is hidden, and recovering something usable from
observations is a large part of the practical work. Chapter 4 is about that
gap.

## What a model buys you

Suppose you have one. What is it for?

**Planning.** With a simulator you can evaluate an action before committing to
it. The canonical form is to search over action sequences and take the one your
model says works out best:

$$
a^{*}_{t:t+H} = \arg\max_{a_{t:t+H}} \; \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{k=0}^{H} \gamma^{k} \, r_\theta(s_{t+k}, a_{t+k}) \right]
$$

You imagine $H$ steps of each candidate future, score them, and act on the
winner. No trial and error in the real world, which matters enormously when
trial and error means crashing a drone.

**Sample efficiency.** Reality is a slow and expensive teacher. A model is a
fast and cheap one, and once it exists you can generate as much synthetic
experience as you have compute for. This is the main practical
argument for model-based methods, and Chapter 6 is entirely about cashing it
in.

**Counterfactuals.** A model answers *what would have happened if I had done
something else*, a question the world itself refuses to answer, because
you only ever get to run the experiment once. Credit assignment, regret, and
most of what we informally call reasoning depend on being able to ask it.

## Two ways to cross a road

Here is the difference, made small enough to watch.

Two identical cars approach two identical walls. The first is a **reflex**: it
brakes when the wall is closer than some fixed distance. Somebody tuned that
distance once, it worked, and it shipped.

The second carries a **model** of its own braking. Every tick it rolls that
model forward (if I brake as hard as I can, starting now, where do I come to
rest?) and it brakes the moment that imagined stopping point ends up
inside the wall.

At low approach speeds the two are indistinguishable, and this is the important
part of the demonstration rather than a warm-up. Reflexes are cheap, fast, and
correct across the whole regime they were tuned in. There is no reason to build
anything more complicated, and evolution and engineering both spend most of
their time here.

Push the speed up and the reflex does not degrade gracefully. It fails at a
threshold, completely, and it fails without any internal signal that something
has changed. The fixed trigger distance encodes an assumption about
speed that nothing in the controller knows it is making. The model car slows
earlier, because $v^2/2a$ grew and it can see that it grew.

<MarginNote>
This is the real dividing line, and it is not &ldquo;model-based is better&rdquo;.
A reflex is a *cached answer*. A model is the thing that lets you compute a new
answer when the question changes. Caches are faster; you just have to know when
yours has gone stale.
</MarginNote>

The model car is not smarter. It is doing something structurally different: it
has a representation of its own dynamics that it can query with hypothetical
inputs. That representation generalises to speeds nobody tuned it for, because
the relationship it captured was the actual one.

## The old idea

None of this is new. It is one of the oldest ideas in the study of mind, and it
keeps being rediscovered by fields that do not read each other.

In 1943 the psychologist Kenneth Craik put it about as well as it has been put:

> If the organism carries a &lsquo;small-scale model&rsquo; of external reality
> and of its own possible actions within its head, it is able to try out
> various alternatives, conclude which is the best of them, react to future
> situations before they arise, utilise the knowledge of past events in dealing
> with the present and the future, and in every way to react in a much fuller,
> safer, and more competent manner to the emergencies which face it.

Control theory got there independently and made it work. The Kalman filter
(1960) maintains an estimate of hidden state by running a forward model of the
system and correcting it against observations: predict, observe, correct, repeat. Apollo's navigation computer ran one, and so does most of the aerospace
industry. The
limitation is that you had to supply the dynamics yourself.

The learning-based line runs through Schmidhuber's work around 1990 on
recurrent networks that model their own environment and are then used for
planning, and through Sutton's Dyna (1991), which interleaves learning from
real experience with learning from experience the model made up. Ha and
Schmidhuber's 2018 paper *World Models*, which is where most people first
met the phrase, put a visual encoder, a recurrent dynamics model, and a
tiny controller together and trained the controller almost entirely inside the
model's hallucinations. That architecture, encoder plus dynamics plus policy,
is still the skeleton of the field.

What changed after 2018 was not the idea. It was that the components got good.
Chapters 6 through 8 are the story of what happens when each piece of that
skeleton gets scaled up: Dreamer for the dynamics, JEPA for the
representation, and video generation models for the whole thing at once.

## Where it gets hard

If it were as easy as Figure 2.1 makes it look, this would be a much shorter
document. Two problems recur, and the rest of the course is organised around
them.

**You do not get the state.** The braking demo handed the model an exact
position and velocity. Real agents get a camera. Somewhere between the pixels
and the prediction, something has to decide what about this scene is *state*
(what will matter later) and throw away the rest. Get that wrong
and the dynamics model is trying to forecast the colour of individual leaves.
Chapters 3, 4 and 7.

**The model is wrong, and rolling it out makes it wronger.** Every prediction
carries error; feeding predictions back in as inputs compounds it. Fifty steps
into an imagined future you are frequently somewhere the training data never
went, being confidently wrong about it. Worse, if you optimise a policy against
that model, the optimiser will hunt for exactly those regions, the places
where the model promises rewards reality will not pay. Chapters 5, 6 and 9.

Most of the modern literature is a response to one or both.
Once you can see that, the papers get much easier to read.

## In short

- A world model is a learned simulator: state and action in, next state out.
- Roll it forward and you can plan, learn cheaply, and ask counterfactuals.
- Reflexes are not inferior. They are cached answers. Models earn their
  cost precisely when conditions leave the range the cache was built for.
- The idea is eighty years old. What is new is that the pieces finally work.
- The two enemies are **representation** (what is state?) and **compounding
  error** (the model is wrong and you keep using it anyway).
