A first serve arrives in about half a second. You are not going to watch it,
decide, and then move.

Work on professional play puts the switch from reacting to anticipating at
somewhere around 140 to 160 milliseconds after the racket hits the ball. In one
study of expert returners, the sideways movement starts at about 177
milliseconds. The player is reading the toss, the shoulder, the racket face and
the first slice of flight, and is committed before the ball has finished saying
where it is going.

That does not prove there is a physics engine in a tennis player's head. It
proves something smaller and more useful. Past a certain speed, acting well
depends on what has not happened yet.

Take what you have. Run it forward. Move for what comes next.

Chapter 1 called that the Dynamics Model, and said it was the definition the
term was coined for. This chapter is what one actually is, what having one buys
you, and the way it fails that no amount of engineering has removed.

One thing to settle first, because it trips people who go on to the papers.
Chapter 1 warned that Ha and Schmidhuber's controller is a separate module from
their world model, and that split holds all the way through here. The model
predicts consequences. Something else reads those predictions and picks.

## What the model actually holds

You saw the shape in Figure 1.3. Where you are, what you do, what comes next. A
dynamics model learns that function, and usually learns what the step was worth
as well.

The part worth slowing down on is what *where you are* is allowed to mean. It
does not have to be a position and a velocity. PlaNet infers it from images and
never names any of it. MuZero goes further and does not reconstruct the
environment at all: its model produces only what its search will consume, which
is reward, value and a policy. TD-MPC2 runs without a decoder <Gloss>the half
of a network that would turn the compressed state back into a picture</Gloss>
at all.

So the model does not have to look like the world. It has to keep enough of
what the world *does*.

<MarginNote>
Which is why "is it photorealistic" is the wrong first question about one of
these. A model of a chess position that cannot draw a bishop is still a
perfectly good model of a chess position.
</MarginNote>

One prediction is not the useful part either. The useful part is that you can
feed the answer back into the front. Hand it a run of actions nobody has taken,
let it read its own output, and you get a future nobody has seen. Chapter 1
called the length of that stretch the horizon.

That is the property to carry, and it is the same one Chapter 1 used to sort
the category. Not how real it looks. Whether you can roll it forward under
actions nobody took, and search over them.

The idea is older than the papers usually cited for it. Thrun, Möller and
Linden were already chaining a learned world model to choose future actions in
1990, in a paper with that name on the front.

## Cached answers

Two cars, two identical walls.

The first brakes when the wall is nearer than some fixed distance. Somebody
tuned that distance once, at the speeds they expected, and shipped it. The
second carries a model of its own braking and asks, every tick, where it would
stop if it braked now.

At the speed the first was tuned for, they do the same thing.

  n="2.1"
  caption={<>Below about forty the two are indistinguishable, and that is the important half of the demonstration. Drag the speed up and watch which one notices. What is being compared is one fixed rule against one thing that recomputes, not model-free against model-based in general.</>}
>
  <BrakingDemo />

Be careful what that picture is comparing. A real model-free policy is not a
fixed rule. It can be a large recurrent network with memory, it can change what
it does based on what it sees, and it can be far cleverer than anything on
screen. Model-free does not mean reflex.

The difference that survives is about when the work got done. A policy has
already spent its training turning experience into answers: *situation,
therefore action*. A model keeps a different object: *situation, plus an action
you are considering, therefore a consequence*. The first is a cache. The second
lets you compute a fresh answer once you know the actual question.

<MarginNote>
A reflex is a *cached answer*. A model is what lets you compute a new one when
the question changes. Caches are faster. You just have to know when yours has
gone stale.
</MarginNote>

Caches are usually right, which is why so much of biology and so much of
engineering is made of them. The interesting part starts when the question
moves outside the range the answer was prepared for.

Arrive faster and the trigger still fires at the same distance. That rule
contains an assumption about how long stopping takes, and nothing inside it
knows it is assuming anything. The model car moves its decision because its
stopping point moved and it can see that it moved.

None of which is free. The model costs computation, has to be learned, and can
be wrong. So real systems sit along a range rather than on one side of a line.
Dyna mixes real experience with experience the model made up. Dreamer learns a
policy from imagination. MuZero pairs a learned model with search.

The question is not whether to carry a model. It is which answers to settle in
advance, and which to leave until you know what you are actually facing.

## What it buys you

Three things, and they are one thing in three costumes. You can compute over
futures that have not happened.

**You can try an action before paying for it.** Put the goal behind an
obstacle. A planner writes down a run of actions, plays it through the model,
scores the imagined result, throws it away and writes another. PlaNet does this
online, in latent space, straight from pixels.

  caption={<>The planner samples action sequences, plays each one through the same model, and keeps the best. Faint lines are the rejects. Raise the budget and the plan improves, but only for as long as the model ranks the candidates honestly. Figure 2.3 is what happens when it stops.</>}
>
  <Planner />
</Figure>

With one candidate it is guessing. With two hundred it is searching. Nothing in
the world changed in between. The improvement is bought entirely with thinking
done between seeing and acting, and a model is the thing that makes that trade
available.

MuZero shows how far it goes. It was never trained to redraw an Atari frame or
lay out a chessboard, and it was never given the rules of chess, Go or shogi.
It learned only the quantities its search needed, and it played all of them
well. A model does not have to reproduce everything that will happen. It has to
keep what the decisions in front of you turn on.

**You can practise where mistakes are cheap.** Sutton's Dyna already ran this
split in 1990: learn a model from real experience, then generate more
experience out of the model and learn from that too. Dreamer put it at the
centre. Learn compact dynamics, imagine long trajectories through them, and
train the behaviour on the imagined ones, so that by the time it has to act there
is nothing left to search. DreamerV3 ran that same recipe across
more than 150 tasks without retuning it per task.

The tempting version of this is *reality is expensive, imagination is free*,
and it is wrong in a way worth fixing. Imagination costs compute, sometimes an
enormous amount of it. What it saves is contact with the world: robot wear,
lab time, supervision, resets, the crash you do not get to undo.

Reality charges you in data. A model lets you pay part of the bill in compute.

**You can ask what if.** What if I brake now, what if I turn instead, what if I
push it from the other side. Comparing those without running them is the whole
reason action conditioning matters. It moves the question from *what probably
happens next* to *what happens if I do this*.

Be exact about what you get. The answer is true inside the model: given what it
has learned, this is what braking earlier would have done. It is not a report
from the world that did not happen. A patch of oil nobody saw, a gust, anything
the model never had, and the answer is confidently wrong.

The world only ever runs the action you took. The model is where the
alternatives get to exist.

## Where it goes wrong

Everything so far argues for more of it. Better model, more candidates, longer
rollouts, pick the winner. Every step is reasonable and the destination is the
characteristic failure of the whole field.

Two things go wrong, and they are not the same thing.

**Errors compound.** Chapter 1 built Figure 1.9 for this one. A model that is
slightly wrong once is fine. A model that is slightly wrong and then reads its
own answer back in is fine for a while and then is nowhere near. Nothing breaks
at any step. A one-step model can be accurate enough to trust while a
hundred-step fantasy stitched out of that same model is worthless. The people
who benchmark these things call the trade the planning horizon dilemma, and it
is why the fix in the next section is as blunt as it is.

**Optimisation goes looking for the errors.** This one is stranger, and it is
the reason the chapter exists.

Say the learned map is wrong in exactly one place: it thinks there is a gap in
a wall that is actually solid. Sample plans at random and you will almost never
go near it. A weak planner never finds it. A strong planner searches properly,
and the best route in the whole learned world runs straight through it.

<Figure
  n="2.3"
  caption={<>The model believes there is a gap in the wall. There is not. Leave the effort low and the planner never finds it, takes the long way round, and gets there. Then turn the effort up. This is a failure the setup makes visible, not a law that more search always hurts.</>}
>
  <Exploitation />
</Figure>

Read that one twice, because it runs backwards through most engineering
instinct. The weaker optimiser produced a plan that worked. The stronger one
produced a plan that scored better and hit a wall. It was not fighting you. It
did exactly what you asked, which was to find the plan the model likes most.

The field calls this **model exploitation**. As the planner gets better at
beating the model, it drifts toward the places the model was never taught, and
those are where its errors live. Offline systems have it worst, because they
cannot go and collect the experience that would correct them.

So a model can have a tiny average error and still be a dangerous thing to plan
inside. Average error asks how it does on the data you measured. Control asks
what happens once something starts hunting, on purpose, for the highest score
it can find. Those are not the same test.

The best plan in the model and the best plan in the world are only the same
plan if the model is good *where the search goes looking*. That clause is the
whole discipline.

<Quote
  who="Sutton"
  context="on planning as computation over a learned model, in the paper that set up the problem"
  where="Dyna, an Integrated Architecture for Learning, Planning and Reacting, 1991"
  href="https://mlanthology.org/icml/1990/sutton1990icml-integrated/"
>
  Planning is the process of taking a model as input and producing or improving
  a policy for interacting with the modelled world.
</Quote>

Exactly right, and the difficulty is sitting in *the modelled world*. What you
get is a policy that is good for the world in the model. Whether that is the
world you are standing in is a separate question, and nothing inside the model
can answer it.

## What people do about it

Nobody fixed this once and moved on. What the field has instead is a set of
ways to limit how far imagination is trusted, and they are all the same
instruction wearing different clothes.

**Ask more than one model.** PETS trains an ensemble <Gloss>several models on
the same data, differing only by how they were initialised and what order they
saw things in</Gloss> and carries their disagreement through the plan. Where
they diverge, the plan is somewhere nothing supports.

Disagreement is evidence, not proof. Models trained on the same gaps can share
the same blind spot and agree confidently about nothing. Work on calibration
found that neural confidence is often simply miscalibrated, and that fixing the
confidence improved the planning.

**Do not dream far.** MBPO's answer is almost rude in its simplicity: start
your imagined rollouts from states the world really produced, and keep them
short. You lose the free infinity of simulation. You gain a limit on how long
an error has to grow before real data interrupts it.

Model-predictive control does the same thing on the clock. Plan a little, act a
little, look again, plan again. Replanning cannot remove a bias the model
carries everywhere, but it does keep handing the world chances to say where
things actually are.

**Charge for uncertainty.** MOPO subtracts a penalty from predicted reward
wherever the model is unsure. An unfamiliar shortcut then has to be good enough
to cover the cost of nobody knowing what is down there.

Underneath all three: *do not let the planner collect full marks for going
somewhere the model has not earned the right to be confident about.*

None of it makes the problem go away. DreamerV3 holds up across an unusually
wide spread of tasks and TD-MPC2 scales to a single agent covering a hundred of
them, and both are careful ways of using a model that is wrong rather than
proof of one that is right.

The question was never how to make the model accurate. It is: accurate where,
for which actions, how far out, against which optimiser, and what should the
thing do when it does not know.
