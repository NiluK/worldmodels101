A professional tennis return is already under way before there is time for the
comfortable version of see ball, decide, move.

Studies of professional play put the boundary between purely reactive behaviour
and anticipation at roughly 140 to 160 milliseconds after the racket hits the
ball. In one experiment with expert returners, sideways movement began around
177 milliseconds after contact. World-class players start organising the return
around the moment of contact itself, reading the server's body and the first
part of the flight. They are not waiting for the finished trajectory and then
responding to it.

That does not prove there is a little physics engine in a tennis player's head.
It makes a narrower point. In a world moving fast enough, useful action depends
on consequences that have not happened yet.

Take what you know now. Run it forward. Move for what comes next.

That is the computational idea behind the learned dynamics model, and this
chapter is about what one actually is, what having one buys you, and why making
the model better has never removed the problem of deciding when to trust it.

Chapter 1 filed this tradition under *the Controller*, because the model is
built to support control. Strictly, the two are different objects. The model
predicts consequences. A planner, a policy or a controller uses those
predictions to choose an action. That separation was explicit in the earliest
neural work and it is still explicit in World Models, PlaNet and Dreamer.

## The model that predicts consequences

Suppose an agent has some internal description of where it is, $z_t$, and is
considering an action, $a_t$. A dynamics model learns something like

$$
\hat p(z_{t+1},\; r_t \mid z_t,\, a_t)
$$

which reads: given what I currently believe about the world, and an action I
might take, what state and what reward should I expect next?

<MarginNote>
*Latent* means learned and internal. Nobody labels those numbers "position" or
"velocity". The network arrives at whatever coordinates make the next
prediction easy, and they are often unreadable to us.
</MarginNote>

The $z$ does not have to be a list of physical quantities. In PlaNet it is a
latent state inferred from images. In MuZero the learned model never
reconstructs the environment at all; it produces the reward, value and policy
information the search needs and nothing else. TD-MPC2 optimises trajectories
inside a latent model with no decoder attached. The model does not have to look
like the world. It has to preserve enough of the world's consequences to choose
well.

One prediction on its own is not much use. What makes it a planning tool is
that you can feed it its own output:

$$
z_t,\, a_t \;\rightarrow\; \hat z_{t+1} \;\rightarrow\; \hat z_{t+2} \;\rightarrow\; \hat z_{t+3} \;\rightarrow\; \cdots
$$

Hand it a sequence of actions nobody has taken, run its predictions back into
itself, and you get an imagined trajectory.

That is the property to carry forward from Chapter 1. Not visual fidelity, and
not even online search by itself. Action-conditioned rolloutability.

PlaNet uses those rollouts at decision time, searching over candidate action
sequences in latent space. MuZero applies its learned model repeatedly inside a
tree search. Dreamer takes a different route: it uses imagined trajectories
mainly to train an actor and a critic, after which the actor can act without
running an expensive search from scratch every time.

So a world model is not defined by whether you can see a planner spinning at
runtime. It is defined here by whether the learned dynamics can answer one
question: *what happens if I do this?*

The lineage is older than the deep learning version. In 1990 Thrun, Möller and
Linden described a learned world model built through interaction and then
chained together to optimise future actions. Sutton's Dyna, from the same
period, alternated learning from the real environment with planning on a model
that was still being learned. Schmidhuber's early model-building controllers
separated a controller from a world model trained to predict the consequences
of that controller's actions.

Ha and Schmidhuber made the modern version memorable in 2018: compress
observations, learn latent dynamics, then train a very small controller on
those dynamics, including experiments where the controller learned entirely
inside the model's generated environment before being moved back to the real
one.

The idea survived because it gives you a new place to compute. Not only in the
world. Before it.

## Cached answers

Two identical cars approach two identical walls.

The first has one rule: brake when the wall is closer than $d$. Somebody chose
$d$, tested it at the expected speeds, and shipped it. The second estimates its
speed, predicts its own motion, and asks on every tick: if I brake now, where do
I stop?

At the speed the first rule was tuned for, they do the same thing.

That last qualification matters more than it looks.

A modern model-free policy can be a large recurrent network. It can carry
memory, adapt to what it sees, and represent a very complicated mapping from
situations to actions. Model-free does not mean stupid, rigid or reactive. The
fixed trigger here is a deliberately simple stand-in, not a fair portrait of the
whole family.

The distinction that survives is about *where the computation happened*. A
policy has already spent training compute turning experience into answers:

$$
\text{situation} \;\rightarrow\; \text{action}
$$

A dynamics model keeps a different reusable object:

$$
\text{situation} + \text{possible action} \;\rightarrow\; \text{possible consequence}
$$

The first is closer to a cache. The second lets part of the decision be
recomputed once the exact problem is known.

<MarginNote>
So keep the line, with the caveat attached. A reflex is a *cached answer*. A
model lets you compute a new answer when the question changes. Caches are
faster. You just have to know when yours has gone stale.
</MarginNote>

Caches are fast, and they are often exactly what you want. The interesting case
begins when the question moves outside the range the answer was prepared for.

Raise the first car's speed and the trigger still fires at the same distance.
That rule contains an assumption about braking distance without containing any
machinery for revising it, and nothing in the controller knows it is making
one. The model car changes its decision because its predicted stopping point
moved and it can see that it moved.

Adaptability is not free. The model costs computation, has to be learned, and
can be wrong. Real systems therefore sit on a spectrum rather than either side
of a line: Dyna mixes real and model-generated experience, Dreamer learns an
actor from imagination, MuZero pairs a learned model with search, TD-MPC2
combines latent dynamics, value learning and model-predictive control.

The question is not *model or no model*. It is: which answers should be
compiled in advance, and which are worth recomputing now?

## What it buys you

Three things, and they are all versions of one capability. You can compute over
futures that have not happened yet.

### You can try an action before paying for it

Suppose the goal sits on the far side of an obstacle. A planner proposes a
sequence of actions, rolls it through the learned dynamics, scores the imagined
trajectory, throws it away and tries another. PlaNet made this practical
straight from pixels. PETS paired probabilistic learned dynamics with sampled
candidate trajectories. TD-MPC2 continues the same idea with local trajectory
optimisation inside a learned implicit model.

With one candidate the planner is close to guessing. With hundreds it can
search. Nothing in the outside world changed while it did so; the extra
performance came from computation between observation and action.

MuZero shows how far that principle goes. Its model is not trained to
reconstruct future Atari frames or reproduce a chessboard. It learns an
iterable representation holding what the tree search needs, and it reached
strong results on Atari, Go, chess and shogi without being given the rules of
any of them.

Which corrects a tempting mistake. A useful dynamics model does not need to
reproduce everything that will happen. It needs to preserve what matters for
the decisions you intend to make.

### You can practise where mistakes are cheap

Dyna already used this split: learn a model from real interaction, then
generate extra imagined transitions and learn from those too. Later Dyna work
calls it imaginary experience outright.

Ha and Schmidhuber pushed the idea into learned neural environments. Dreamer
put it at the centre of the algorithm: learn compact latent dynamics, imagine
trajectories through them, train an actor and critic on those imagined futures.
DreamerV3, published in Nature in 2025, ran one configuration of that algorithm
across more than 150 tasks.

The tempting sentence here is *reality is expensive, imagination is free*. It is
memorable and wrong in one important way. Imagination costs compute, sometimes
a great deal of it.

What a model saves is environment interaction: robot wear, experiment time,
human supervision, resets, safety risk, or simply the number of real
transitions you have to collect. PETS was built around exactly that
sample-efficiency argument, and DreamerV3 reports strong data efficiency while
being open about the compute it trades away for it.

So the better line is this. Reality charges you for data, and a model lets you
pay part of the bill in compute instead.

### You can ask what if

What if I brake now? What if I turn left instead? What if I push the object
from the other side?

A dynamics model can compare those without running all of them, which is
exactly why action conditioning matters. It changes the question from *what is
likely to happen next* into *what does my model predict if I do this*.

Be precise about what that gives you. A learned model answers a model-relative
hypothetical: under what I have learned, what would happen if I braked earlier?
That is not the same as knowing what the physical world would truly have done.
An unseen patch of oil, a gust of wind or any other hidden variable can make
the answer wrong.

The world only runs the action you actually take. The model is where the
alternatives get to exist.

And that leads straight to the problem.

## Where imagination goes wrong

Everything above reads as an argument for more planning. Learn a better model.
Try more possibilities. Roll further ahead. Pick the best one.

Each step sounds sensible on its own. Together they produce the characteristic
failure of model-based control. There are two errors here, and it helps not to
collapse them into one.

### Errors compound

A learned model is never exactly right. Over a single step a small error may
stay small. But planning requires each prediction to become the input to the
next:

$$
z_t \;\rightarrow\; \hat z_{t+1} \;\rightarrow\; \hat z_{t+2} \;\rightarrow\; \cdots \;\rightarrow\; \hat z_{t+H}
$$

The model is now conditioning mostly on states it generated itself rather than
states the real environment produced.

Janner and colleagues made that trade-off the centre of Model-Based Policy
Optimization: longer rollouts give you more synthetic experience but expose the
policy to more accumulated model bias, which argues for short rollouts branched
off real data. A broad 2019 benchmark of model-based methods found the same
thing and named it the planning horizon dilemma.

So a one-step model can be accurate enough to be useful while a hundred-step
fantasy built by applying that same model over and over is hopeless. There need
not be one spectacular mistake. Only small ones with descendants.

### Optimisation finds the mistakes

Compounding error is bad. Optimisation makes it stranger.

Say the learned map holds one small error: it believes there is a gap in a wall
where the real wall is solid. Most random action sequences never go near that
gap, and a weak planner may never find it. A strong planner searches much more
thoroughly, and eventually it finds the best route in the entire learned world.

Straight through the wall.

Read that twice. A weaker optimiser produced an action that worked. A stronger
one produced an action that is better according to the model and worse in
reality. The optimiser is not fighting you. It is doing precisely what you
asked.

The field calls this **model exploitation**. As a planner or policy improves
against the learned model, it drifts towards actions and states where the model
has little supporting data and larger errors. MBPO analyses that tension
directly. Offline work such as MOPO makes it even more visible, because there
the agent cannot go and collect real experience to correct itself.

This is the part that breaks ordinary engineering intuition. Optimisation
usually means taking the objective more seriously. But when the objective is
scored through an imperfect stand-in for reality, taking the stand-in more
seriously is a way of finding its flaws. The planner does not merely suffer
model error. It searches for it, by accident.

Which is why a model with a tiny average prediction error can still be a
dangerous planning model. Average accuracy asks how well the model predicts the
distribution you measured it on. Control asks what happens once an optimiser
starts deliberately hunting for unusual, high-scoring actions. Those are
different tests.

The whole failure fits on one line:

$$
\underset{a}{\operatorname{argmax}}\, \text{predicted outcome} \;\neq\; \underset{a}{\operatorname{argmax}}\, \text{real outcome}
$$

unless the model is good enough *where the optimiser chooses to go*. That last
clause is the field.

<Quote
  who="Sutton"
  context="on planning as computation over a learned model, in the paper that set up the whole problem"
  where="Dyna, an Integrated Architecture for Learning, Planning and Reacting, 1991"
  href="https://mlanthology.org/icml/1990/sutton1990icml-integrated/"
>
  Planning is the process of taking a model as input and producing or improving
  a policy for interacting with the modelled world.
</Quote>

Which is exact, and carries the whole difficulty in the phrase *the modelled
world*. The policy you get is good for the world in the model. Whether that is
the world you are standing in is a separate question, and nothing inside the
model can answer it.

## How the field contains the error

Nobody solved model exploitation once and moved on. Model-based reinforcement
learning instead accumulated ways of restricting where, and how far, imagination
is trusted.

**Ask several models.** PETS uses an ensemble of probabilistic dynamics models
and carries their uncertainty through candidate trajectories. Disagreement
between them is evidence that a proposed plan has wandered into territory
nothing much supports.

But disagreement is evidence, not an oracle. Several models trained on the same
biased data can agree and still be wrong. Work on calibrated model-based
reinforcement learning showed that neural predictive uncertainty is itself often
inaccurate, and that calibrating it improved planning, precisely because raw
confidence should not be taken at face value.

**Do not dream too far.** MBPO's answer is almost aggressively simple: start
imagined rollouts from real states, and keep them short. You give up some of the
attractive infinity of simulation. In return you keep anchoring imagination near
states the real environment actually produced, which limits how long an error
has to compound before reality gets a say.

Model-predictive control applies a related idea at decision time. Plan a
little, act a little, look again, re-plan. PlaNet re-plans in latent space as
new observations arrive; TD-MPC2 optimises locally rather than committing to one
enormous imagined future. Re-planning cannot remove systematic bias, but it
gives the real world repeated chances to correct where the model thinks things
are.

**Penalise suspicious futures.** MOPO makes pessimism explicit for the offline
case. It learns dynamics, estimates uncertainty, and subtracts a penalty from
predicted reward wherever that uncertainty is high. An unfamiliar shortcut then
has to be not merely good in imagination, but good enough to pay the cost of
not knowing what is there.

The implementations differ. The instruction underneath them does not: *do not
let the planner take full credit for going somewhere the model has not earned
the right to be confident about.*

None of it makes the problem disappear. DreamerV3 shows that learned world
models now hold up across an unusually wide set of control tasks, and
TD-MPC2 shows that implicit latent models scale to large multi-task continuous
control. Those are real advances. Neither turns learned dynamics into ground
truth. Both are careful ways of using a model without needing it to reproduce
the world.

The problem was never *how do I make the model accurate*. It is: accurate
where, for which actions, over what horizon, under which optimiser, and what
should the agent do when it does not know?

## What to carry into Chapter 3

- A dynamics model and a controller are not the same object. The model predicts
  the consequences of actions; a planner or policy uses those predictions.
  Chapter 1 grouped this lineage under control for functional reasons, not
  architectural ones.
- A cached answer is an analogy, not a definition of model-free learning. Fixed
  reflexes make the trade-off easy to see, but modern policies can be very rich.
  What a model adds is the ability to recompute consequences for candidate
  actions after the current problem is known.
- The defining capability is action-conditioned rolloutability, not
  photorealism. PlaNet plans in a compact stochastic latent state, MuZero plans
  with an abstract model of reward, policy and value, TD-MPC2 uses no decoder at
  all. None of them needs a film of the future in order to control it.
- Optimisation changes the error distribution. A planner does not sample the
  model innocently; it hunts for whatever the model says is best, which can
  drive it straight at the model's weakest regions. Ensembles, calibrated
  uncertainty, short rollouts and pessimism are all ways of controlling that
  trust.

Everything here quietly assumed the model already had a useful state to roll
forward. A position. A velocity. A compact vector holding whatever the future
depends on.

In the real problem nobody hands you one. You get pixels.

Thanks for reading. Chapter 3 is about why an objective that sounds almost
insultingly simple, *predict what comes next*, can force a model to invent the
hidden state it needs.
