Yann LeCun was Meta's chief AI scientist at the time, and he is a Turing Award winner. He is one of
the people who made convolutional networks work. In 2022 he published *A Path
Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence*, a position paper on how machines
should learn. One charge in it was aimed at the reflex then governing learned
world models: when in doubt, predict the next picture.

Point a camera at a road and ask what the next second looks like. A leaf at the
edge of the frame will move, and which way is unknowable. It is also the least
useful fact on offer, since nothing you might do about the road depends on it.
A model asked to predict the picture has to predict that leaf anyway.

The leaf is made of pixels, and pixels are what the model is marked on.
Multiply that by every leaf, every ripple, every flicker of light on wet
tarmac. Most of the capacity has gone to things that are hard to predict and do
not matter. That is the leaf problem, the smaller of the two charges.

## A deterministic average of two futures is neither

The larger charge is a car coming to a junction. It will turn left or right.
You do not know which, and no amount of staring at the current frame will tell
you.

Ask a deterministic model, one that gives a single answer, for one next
picture. Score it by squared pixel error, the gap at each pixel squared and
added up. The best answer in the maths is the conditional average of the two
turns: the left and right futures blended at their odds, rather than either one
on its own.

The middle panel scores better than either real future. A model that predicted
the left turn would be punished half the time. The ghost in the middle is
punished a little all the time, and under squared error a little all the time
wins.

That training signal asks for the mean, not for a plausible future. When the
future is open, the mean is a smear that never occurs.

<MarginNote>
A deterministic predictor trained with a mean-seeking pixel loss goes vague
just when something interesting is about to happen. Modern generators avoid
that failure by drawing samples rather than aiming at a mean.
</MarginNote>

## The ghost had an answer five years early

Pixel prediction had dealt with the ghost by 2017, five years before the
position paper. SV2P, short for *Stochastic Variational Video Prediction*, is
Mohammad Babaeizadeh and colleagues' video predictor from that year. Emily
Denton and Rob Fergus built a similar one. Both learn a spread of possible
futures and draw one at random.

The trick is a hidden variable, a few numbers standing in for whatever the
frame cannot tell you, such as which way the car turns. Draw the variable, then
the frame that goes with it. Each draw can be sharp even when the model has no
idea which turn will happen.

  caption={<>Switch from the mean to samples of the same two futures and the in-between frame disappears. Press draw a few times. The uncertainty survives as variation across draws. The model still does not say which draw the world will choose, so a planner has to value risk across the whole spread.</>}
>

</Figure>

That kills the blur objection, and anyone still running it is arguing with
2017. It does not kill the rest of the case.

Sampling fixes the form of the output. It does not make useless texture cheap,
make rare futures well estimated, or tell a decision maker which possible
future matters. Stochastic generation keeps the target. Embedding prediction
changes it, and that is the real line between the two camps.

## Predict the description instead

LeCun's proposal is to stop predicting the picture. Encode what you can see
into a compact description <Gloss>an *embedding*, a short list of numbers that
stands in for something bigger</Gloss>. Similar things land near each other in
that list. Encode the part you are predicting into one too.

Then train the model to predict the second description from the first, and
never decode anything back into pixels. I-JEPA, a 2023 image model from Mahmoud
Assran and colleagues in LeCun's group at Meta, is the idea at its cleanest.
Hide part of the picture and predict the embedding of the missing piece.

V-JEPA 2, from Meta AI in 2025, does the same for video. It is trained first on
plain video, with no actions, then again on a robot's actions, for control.
That is where the argument meets a robot.

This buys two things. The leaf problem goes away. The description never had to
record which way the leaf went, and if a detail does not survive the encoding,
nothing downstream is graded on it.

The ghost shrinks as well, because you are no longer averaging pictures. The
descriptions of a car turning left and one turning right can share most of
their coordinates. The part still open is either kept explicit or dropped as
irrelevant. A space of descriptions can still hold two separate answers,
though, and changing the representation does not get rid of uncertainty.

<MarginNote>
This is the JEPA family, and the name is the architecture read aloud. It is
joint embedding because both sides get embedded, and predictive because the
training signal is one embedding predicting another.
</MarginNote>

## The bill for that

Predicting pixels has one large virtue, and the JEPA side gives it up. The
target is fixed. The next frame is the next frame, and the model cannot make it
easier. Predict an embedding and that stops being true.

The target now comes from the encoder, the network that turns pictures into
descriptions, which is also being trained. It can get a perfect score while
learning nothing, by making every description identical. The prediction is then
always right, the loss goes to zero, and the representation says nothing about
anything. That failure is called collapse.

The useful run is the one that looks worse.</>}

Everything hard about these methods lives in preventing that, and the fixes are
a lineage. In 2020 the received wisdom for learning descriptions without labels
was contrastive. Pull two views of the same image together, and push everything
else apart so the descriptions cannot all pile up in one place.

BYOL, short for *Bootstrap Your Own Latent*, a 2020 method from Jean-Bastien
Grill and colleagues at DeepMind, dropped the pushing apart. It keeps a second
copy of the encoder that updates slowly <Gloss>usually called a *target
encoder*, it trails the one being trained</Gloss>, and predicts that copy's
output. The target cannot run away from the predictor.

By the standard account it should have collapsed. It did not, and that
convinced people collapse could be avoided without pushing anything apart.

VICReg, a 2021 method from Adrien Bardes, Jean Ponce and LeCun, went the
explicit route instead. The name is the recipe read aloud: variance,
invariance, covariance. It penalises descriptions whose parts have collapsed or
copied each other, which is Figure 7.3's safeguard done properly.

Randall Balestriero and colleagues' 2023 survey, *A Cookbook of Self-Supervised
Learning*, is frank that much of the field is machinery for stopping the
trivial solution from winning. None of it is elegant. With the model grading
its own homework, I doubt it ever will be.

The loss is also no longer a number you can read. A pixel loss of zero means
the model predicted the frame. An embedding loss of zero might mean it
understands everything, or nothing at all. The number will not tell you which.

So Quentin Garrido and colleagues tested what had been learned. They checked
what the embeddings could do. Their 2024 paper is *Learning and Leveraging
World Models in Visual Representation Learning*.

## What this does not settle

None of this makes generation a mistake. The version of the argument that says
it does is the overclaim. Systems that predict pixels are the ones making
worlds you can walk around in, and they work.

Whatever they waste on leaves, they deliver a picture, and no embedding does
that. To see the future rather than reason about it, somebody has to draw it.

The awkward counter-example comes from LeCun's own lab. Kaiming He, best known
for ResNet, the network design computer vision ran on for years, was at that
lab in 2021. His paper that year, *Masked Autoencoders Are Scalable Vision
Learners*, hides most of an image and rebuilds its pixels, the thing JEPA
refuses to do.

The descriptions it learns are very good anyway, and it came out the year
before the position paper. I keep that result next to every JEPA claim.

The version of the argument that holds up is narrower than the slogan. The
narrow version is right. Predicting appearances spends most of its capacity on
things no decision depends on. When the future is uncertain, a deterministic
predictor is trained towards an image of something that will not happen.

If what you want is a compact state to act on, that is a bad way to get one. If
what you want is a picture, it is the only way anyone has. Most of the
confusion in this corner of the field comes from comparing systems built for
different goals.
