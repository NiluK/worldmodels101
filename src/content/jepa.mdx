Point a camera at a road and ask what the next second looks like.

A leaf on a tree at the edge of the frame will move. Which way it moves is
unknowable, and it is also the least interesting fact available. Nothing you
might decide to do depends on it.

But a model asked to predict the picture has to predict that leaf, because the
leaf is made of pixels and pixels are what it is being marked on. Multiply that by every leaf, every ripple, every flicker of light on wet
tarmac. Most of the capacity in the system has now gone to things that are hard
to predict and do not matter.

That is the smaller of the two problems.

## The average of two futures is neither of them

Here is the larger one.

A car is coming to a junction. It will turn left or it will turn right. You
genuinely do not know which, and no amount of looking harder at the current
frame will tell you.

Ask a model to predict the next picture and score it by how close the pixels
are, and there is a mathematically best answer. It is not either turn. It is
the average of them.

  n="7.1"
  caption={<>The middle panel is not an impression of blurriness. It is the two futures drawn at their probabilities, which is their average, which is exactly what squared error is minimised by. Slide the chance towards either end and the optimum resolves; leave it near the middle and the best available prediction is a picture of something that cannot happen.</>}

Read that middle panel again. It scores better than either of the real futures.
A model that confidently predicted the left turn would be punished half the
time; the ghost is punished a little all the time, and a little all the time
wins.

So the training signal is not asking for a plausible future. It is asking for
the mean of the futures, and when the future is genuinely open, the mean is a
smear that never occurs.

<MarginNote>
This is why generated video gets vague exactly when something interesting is
about to happen. Uncertainty is where the smear comes from, and uncertainty is
concentrated at the moments that matter.
</MarginNote>

## Predict the description instead

The proposal is short. Stop predicting the picture.

Encode what you can see into a compact description <Gloss>an *embedding*: a
short list of numbers standing in for something bigger, arranged so that
similar things land near each other</Gloss>. Encode the part you are predicting
into one too. Then train the model to predict the
second description from the first, and never decode anything back into pixels
at all.

This buys two things at once.

The leaf problem goes away, because the description was never obliged to record
which way the leaf went. If a detail does not survive the encoding, nothing
downstream is graded on it.

The averaging problem gets smaller, because you are no longer averaging
pictures. The description of a car turning left and the description of a car turning
right can share almost everything. What is being predicted may be a single
point that both of them are close to, so the prediction has somewhere sensible
to land.

<MarginNote>
This is the JEPA family, and the name says the architecture: joint embedding,
because both sides get embedded, and predictive, because the training signal is
one embedding predicting another.
</MarginNote>

## The bill for that

Now the part that is usually skipped, and it is not a small part.

Predicting pixels has one enormous virtue. The target is fixed. The next frame
is the next frame, and the model cannot make it easier.

Predict an embedding and that stops being true, because the thing you are
predicting is produced by a network that is also being trained. There is now a
way to get a perfect score that involves learning nothing at all: make every
description identical. The prediction is then always exactly right, the loss
goes to zero, and the representation says nothing about anything.

<Figure
  n="7.2"
  caption={<>A two-by-two encoder trained on four pairs by gradient descent. Nothing here is illustrative: the numbers are whatever the arithmetic produces. Without the safeguard the loss reaches zero and the representation reaches zero with it. Turn the safeguard on and the loss never gets close to zero, which is the point. The useful run is the one that looks worse.</>}
>
  <Collapse />
</Figure>

Everything difficult about these methods lives in preventing that. Keep a second
copy of the encoder that updates slowly <Gloss>usually called a *target
encoder*: it trails the one being trained, so the thing being predicted cannot
sprint away from the thing predicting it</Gloss>, so the target cannot run away.
Push different inputs apart explicitly. Penalise descriptions whose components
all say the same thing. There are several families of answer and none of them
is elegant.

Notice what has happened to the loss. It is no longer a measurement you can
read. A pixel loss of zero means the model predicted the frame. An embedding loss of
zero might mean it understands everything, or nothing at all, and the number
will not tell you which. The score stopped being trustworthy at the same
moment it stopped being about pixels.

## What this does not settle

It would be tidy to end with generation being a mistake. It is not.

Systems that predict pixels are the ones producing worlds you can walk around
in, and they work. Whatever is being wasted on leaves, they are delivering
something that no amount of embedding prediction delivers, which is an actual
picture. If you want to see the future rather than reason about it, somebody
has to draw it.

So the honest statement of the argument is narrower than the slogan. Predicting
appearances spends most of its capacity on things no decision depends on. And
when the future is uncertain, it is trained towards an image of something that
will not happen. If what you want is a compact state to act on, that is a bad
way to get one. If what you want is a picture, it is the only way anyone has.

The two goals were never the same goal. Most of the confusion in this corner of
the field is people comparing systems that were built for different ones.
