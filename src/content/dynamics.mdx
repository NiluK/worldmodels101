A model that predicts one step ahead is easy to be impressed by.

Show it where things are, ask what happens next, compare its answer to what
happened. Do that ten thousand times and read off the average error. The number
will be small, and it will be honest, and it will not tell you what you want to
know.

Because you were never going to use it one step at a time. You were going to
ask it for a hundred steps, and on step two it stops being given the truth and
starts being given its own answer.

At four per cent error per step, corrected, it is still hugging the truth after
thirty steps. Left alone, it ends up thirty-nine times further away. Same model.
Same mistake. The only thing that changed is what it was handed at the start of
each step.

## The mismatch

This has a name, and the name is unhelpfully technical for something so
simple. During training, a sequence model is usually shown the true previous
value at every step, because that is what the recording contains and it makes
the training stable. That is called **teacher forcing**.

The trouble is that nobody teacher-forces you in production. At the moment of
use the model gets its own last answer, which is slightly wrong, and it has
never once been asked to recover from being slightly wrong.

<MarginNote>
So it is not that the model is bad at long horizons. It is that it was trained
on a distribution of inputs it will never see again the moment you start
rolling it out, and nothing in the training loop noticed.
</MarginNote>

Look at that figure again and notice what the grey line is really telling you.
It is not a measure of how good the model is. It is a measure of how good the
model is at a job it will never be asked to do.

## What has to happen at each step

Strip the machinery away and a transition model does one thing: take what you
know, take what you did, and produce what you know next.

The awkward part is the first of those. What you know is not just the last
picture. It is everything relevant that has ever happened, folded into
something small enough to carry. A ball behind a wall is still moving. A door
you opened four rooms ago is still open.

So the real question is how the past gets carried, and there are two answers.

**Carry a summary.** Keep one fixed block of numbers. Every time something new
arrives, fold it in and throw the old block away. The work per step never
changes, however long the sequence gets. This is what recurrent networks do
<Gloss>a network with a loop in it: each step's output is handed back as part
of the next step's input</Gloss>, and what the state-space models that have
come back into fashion do too.

**Keep everything.** Store every step, and when a new one arrives, look back
over all of them and decide what is relevant. Nothing gets thrown away, because
nothing has to be summarised. This is attention <Gloss>for each new step, score every earlier step for
relevance, then read mostly from the ones that scored highest</Gloss>, and it
is what transformers do.

The trade underneath is not really about speed. A fixed summary has to decide,
at every step, what is worth keeping, and it has to decide without knowing what
will be needed later. Attention never has to decide, which is exactly why it
never compresses.

It is the same tension as the squeeze between a camera and a decision, one
level up. Something has to be thrown away, and the thing throwing it away does
not know the future.

## Splitting the difference

A useful trick, and one worth knowing because it shows up in the systems that
work.

Carry two things forward rather than one. One part is computed the same way
every time, and holds whatever reliably follows from the last state. The other
is sampled, and holds whatever could still go either way. A ball's motion goes
in the first. Whether the door opens goes in the second.

Keep only the deterministic part and the model cannot represent genuine
uncertainty, so it confidently invents one future. Keep only the stochastic
part and it struggles to remember anything for long, because noise gets
injected at every step. PlaNet's argument for carrying both is that each one
fails alone in a different direction.

## Living with it

Nobody has removed the mismatch. What the field has instead is four ways of
not being destroyed by it.

**Train it on its own output.** If the problem is that the model never
practised recovering from its own mistakes, let it make some during training.
Feed it its own predictions part of the time, and raise that fraction as it
improves. The usual name for this is *scheduled sampling*.

**Train the rollout, not the step.** Instead of scoring one-step accuracy,
score a whole imagined stretch against a whole real one, so the thing being
optimised is the thing you will actually use.

**Do not go far.** Keep the rollouts short and start them from real states.
Cheap, effective, and it gives up the part of the appeal that was infinite.

**Look again.** Predict a few steps, act on one, take a fresh observation, and
start over. This is most of robotics, and it works because a real measurement
resets the accumulated error to nothing.

None of these is a fix. They are all the same admission: a learned transition
model is trustworthy over a horizon, and part of the engineering is knowing how
long that horizon is.
