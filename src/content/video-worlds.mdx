Take a model that predicts the next frame of video, and add one input.

Alongside the frames it has already seen, tell it which key is being held. Now the
same opening gives you one continuation if you hold left and a different one if
you hold right. It has stopped being a video and started being somewhere you
are.

That is the entire architectural move. Everything else in this chapter is what
happens when you do it at scale.

## What scaling bought

More than most people expected, including most of the people building it.

Genie 3 is reported to generate interactive video at 720p and 24 frames a
second. It is reported to hold a scene together over several minutes, and to
take instructions part-way through: change the weather, add a flock of birds.
Walk away from something, walk back, and it is roughly as you left it.

<MarginNote>
Take those numbers as the lab's report rather than as measurements anyone
outside it has repeated. That is not a slight; it is the normal state of this
subject, and Chapter 9 is largely about how little of it can currently be
checked.
</MarginNote>

Nothing in that system stores the scene. There is no geometry behind the
picture, no list of objects, nothing you could export. The room stays put
because the model got extremely good at continuing what it had already drawn,
and continuing consistently is part of continuing well.

Which is a stranger achievement than it sounds. A system with no memory of a
room, reconstructing that room from its own previous output, well enough that
you cannot catch it out in a minute of looking.

## What "emergent physics" means

You will see the claim made about every one of these systems. It is worth
being precise about what it can and cannot mean.

The observation behind it is real. Train on enough video and the outputs start respecting things nobody put in.
Dropped objects accelerate downwards. Water pools rather than piling up. Things
hidden behind other things come back when the thing in front moves. No equation was supplied. Those regularities are in the
training footage, and predicting the footage well requires reproducing them.

The claim built on top is where the trouble starts. "It learned physics"
suggests it acquired the rule, and there is a test for that. It is not whether
the outputs look right on the sort of thing it was trained on.

<Figure
  n="8.2"
  caption={<>The dashed line is a model fitted to the true behaviour across one band of speeds. Inside that band it is within a few per cent, and anyone testing it there would conclude it had the rule. Drag outside the band. It does not have the rule; it has a straight line through the part of the curve it was shown.</>}
>
  <EmergentPhysics />
</Figure>

Everything inside the band is consistent with having learned physics. It is
equally consistent with having learned the answers to the questions it was
asked. The two only come apart outside, and outside is exactly where nobody
tests, because outside is where you have no footage to compare against.

So the honest version of the claim is narrower and more useful. These systems
reproduce a great many physical regularities across the range of situations
they were trained on. Whether that constitutes having the rule is not settled
by any demonstration you have seen, because the demonstrations are all inside
the band.

<MarginNote>
This is not a special failing of video models. It is the ordinary problem with
any fitted function, and the reason it feels different here is only that the
outputs are so persuasive. A wrong answer that looks like a photograph is more
convincing than a wrong number.
</MarginNote>

## What they are for

Given all that, it would be easy to be dismissive, and that would be a mistake.

For training other systems, a generated world is very useful. It costs nothing
to run. It resets instantly. It will produce a thousand variations of a scene
that would take a week to stage. NVIDIA's Cosmos is
built and marketed on exactly this: physically-aware generated video as
training data for robots and vehicles.

For being a world you can move around in, they are already good. That is worth
something on its own, and no other approach in this course delivers it.

For being the thing an agent plans inside, they are the wrong shape. A planner
needs to try a hundred action sequences and score them, which means running the
model a hundred times, and running these models is expensive. It also needs a compact state to reason over, and a frame is the opposite of
compact.

## Where the seams are

Three, briefly, because the next chapter is the full accounting.

**Consistency is learned, not enforced.** Nothing prevents the room from
changing. It usually does not, and the fact that it usually does not is a
statistical property of a trained system rather than a guarantee.

**The interesting moments are the vague ones.** A model trained on pixels is
pushed towards the average of the possible futures wherever the future is open.
The future is most open exactly when something is about to happen.

**Nobody agrees how to score them.** There is no accepted measurement of
whether a generated world is behaving, which means claims about improvement are
mostly claims about how the outputs look to the person making them.
