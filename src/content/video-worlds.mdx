Until 2024 a video model was something you watched. It chose its own future and
the audience had no say. One extra input changed that.

Take a model that predicts the next frame and tell it, along with the frames,
which key is being held. It can now give the same opening one continuation for
left and another for right.

Jake Bruce and colleagues at Google DeepMind made that move with Genie in 2024,
in a paper called *Genie: Generative Interactive Environments*. Genie was
trained on footage of 2D platform games (Mario and its descendants) and turned
it into worlds you could steer. Nobody had labelled the keypresses.

The model had to explain each change between frames with one of a handful of
codes, and the codes it settled on behaved like a controller. That is what
turns a video model into somewhere you can be. It also married two families
that had grown up apart: world models built for agents (chapters 5 and 6) and
video models built for audiences.

The input is needed for an interactive world. It does not, on its own, prove
the model obeys it, and the announcements tend to skip that question.

## An action input is not control

A model can receive an action and still ignore it, or respond weakly. It can
also let the action change how things look without changing the path
underneath. A steering wheel can turn without being connected to anything.

That failure is the one to expect. A model trained on footage is rewarded for
frames that look right. If the footage mostly goes straight, going straight is
a good bet whatever the key says.

Control has to be measured. Hold the start fixed, vary only the command, and
check whether the futures split apart as asked. Call it the steering-wheel test.

  n="8.2"
  caption={<>Every row gets a different command from the same start. This is the steering-wheel test as a picture. Conditioning is the name for giving the model the action as an input. Action fidelity is how far the model obeys it. Drag fidelity towards zero and the paths fold together while the input stays present.</>}
>

Video quality is the best disguise weak control has. If three beautiful
continuations all go straight, you have been shown video. A planner that asked
for three counterfactuals, three what-ifs from one start, has been handed one
of them three times.

## From watching to walking in

Scaling bought more than most people expected, including most of the people
building it.

GameNGen came from Dani Valevski and colleagues at Google Research in 2024.
Their paper was called *Diffusion Models Are Real-Time Game Engines*. A
diffusion model makes a picture by starting from noise and cleaning it up step
by step. GameNGen used one to run DOOM, the first-person shooter from the early
nineties.

It made each frame from the earlier frames and the player's inputs, fast
enough to play. People shown short clips struggled to tell it from the real
game, its authors reported. That is where watching ended and playing began.

Genie 3, from Google DeepMind a year later, is where the numbers stopped
looking like a demo. It is reported to make interactive video at 720p and 24
frames a second.

It is said to hold a scene together for several minutes and to take
instructions part-way through: change the weather, add a flock of birds. Walk
away from something, walk back, and it is roughly as you left it.

<MarginNote>
Those numbers are DeepMind's report of its own system. Nobody outside the lab
has repeated them. That is normal here rather than a slight.
</MarginNote>

The public interface offers no stored 3D layout of the scene, and no list of
its objects, that you could inspect. Yet DeepMind says Genie 3 can look back
over its own path and keep visual information for about a minute. Whatever
state supports that stays hidden inside the generator. The behaviour shows
persistence without showing what carries it.

In under two years the field went from steering a blurry platformer to
walking around a photoreal scene that remembered where you had been. What
scaling did not buy was anything you could open up and read.

## Emergent physics, and where the footage runs out

You will see the claim made about all of them. OpenAI's 2024 report, *Video
generation models as world simulators*, put emergent physics into common use.
It was the write-up for Sora, OpenAI's own video generator. Its title did most
of the work, and the claim it spread bundles an observation with a conclusion.

The observation is real. Train on enough video and the outputs start to respect
regularities nobody put in. Dropped objects speed up as they fall, water pools
rather than piling up, and hidden objects come back into view when the thing in
front moves. Nobody supplied an equation; those regularities are in the
training footage, and predicting it well means copying them.

The conclusion is where the trouble starts. "It learned physics" says the model
picked up the rule. That is testable. The test has never been whether the
outputs look right on the footage it was trained on.

Bingyi Kang and colleagues ran one in 2024, in a paper called *How Far is Video
Generation from World Model: A Physical Law Perspective*. They trained video
models on simple simulated scenes of balls moving and colliding, then asked for
speeds the footage never showed. Figure 8.3 is the shape of what they found.

  caption={<>The dashed line is a model fitted to the true behaviour across one band of speeds. Inside the band it sits within a few per cent, so anyone testing there would credit it with the rule. Drag the speed outside the band and it carries on straight while the true curve bends away. It has a line through the part of the curve it was shown.</>}
>
  <EmergentPhysics />

Everything inside the band fits with having learned physics. It fits just as
well with having learned the answers to the questions it was asked. Kang's
group concluded that their models were matching each new scene to the training
scenes it most resembled, and copying what happened there.

Nobody tests outside the band, because that is where the footage runs out. Call
that the band problem.

The honest version of the claim is narrower. These systems copy a great many
physical regularities across the situations they were trained on. Whether that
is the same as having the rule, no demo you have seen can settle. Every one of
them was inside the band.

<MarginNote>
The band problem is not special to video. Any fitted function has it. It feels
different here only because a wrong answer that looks like a photograph
convinces people in a way a wrong number never could.
</MarginNote>

## What they are for

None of that argues against using them. Answering "it has not learned physics"
with "so it is a toy" is the mirror-image error.

For training other systems a generated world is useful. It avoids contact with
the real environment and pays in GPU time instead. It resets at once and can
produce a thousand versions of a scene that would take a week to stage.

NVIDIA, whose chips train most of these models, built Cosmos for this. It is a
set of video generators described in a 2025 paper, *Cosmos World Foundation
Model Platform for Physical AI*. It makes physically-aware video as training
data for robots and vehicles, where real data is dearest.

NVIDIA sells Cosmos beside its explicit, hand-built simulators as one platform,
and has not declared either the replacement for the other.

For being a world you can move around in, they are already good. No other
approach lets you walk into the picture. I think that is worth
more than the sceptics admit and less than the launches imply.

For being the model an agent plans inside, raw video generators are an awkward
shape. A planner may need to score a hundred action sequences, and rendering
each one is expensive. Systems can instead distil a learned latent, a short
internal code that stands in for the frames, and plan in that. Then the latent
state does the planning, not the visible video.

## Where the seams are

There are three.

**Consistency is learned, not enforced.** Nothing stops the room from changing.
It usually does not. But usually is a statistical property of a trained system,
and a guarantee is a property of an engine.

**The interesting moments are the vague ones.** A model trained on pixels is
pushed towards the average of the possible futures wherever the future is open.
The future is most open when something is about to happen. The steering-wheel
test is hardest to pass where passing it matters.

**The scores do not compose.** Benchmarks measure visual quality, geometry,
action fidelity and persistence one at a time. There is no accepted rule for
combining them into one ranking. I will not tell you whether Genie 3 beats
GameNGen, and you should distrust anyone who does. A claim about improvement
belongs to one particular test.
