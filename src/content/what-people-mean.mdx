Somebody says it in a meeting and you nod.

Ten minutes later there are five tabs open and they describe five different
machines. One generates video you steer with the arrow keys. One exports
geometry into Blender. One is a paper about predicting embeddings that never
shows you a video at all.

That is not a research failure. Between roughly 2018 and 2024, computer vision,
reinforcement learning, robotics and interpretability all reached for the same
two words, and every one of them was describing something real. By 2026 the
labs had started publishing dictionaries.

In this chapter I'll deal with the five senses in current use, where each came
from, and the one test that separates them. Chapters 2 through 6 build the
machinery underneath.

## Origins

The term started somewhere specific. In control theory, and later in
reinforcement learning <Gloss>training an agent by letting it act, watching what
happens, and rewarding what worked</Gloss>, a world model is a learned
transition function. A state and an action go in. The next state comes out.

Written down it looks like this:

$$
p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)
$$

Read it left to right. The chance of the next state $s_{t+1}$, given the state
you are in now $s_t$, and the action you take $a_t$. That is the whole object.
Most of the arguments in this field are about what to put in place of $s$.

Kalman's 1960 filter nailed one half of it: working out a hidden state from
noisy measurements <Gloss>it estimates state, but never asks what happens if I
act, so it is ancestry rather than membership</Gloss>. Around 1990 Schmidhuber
built neural systems in two parts, where one network modelled the world and
another chose actions, and the first predicted what the second's choices would
do. Sutton's Dyna did something related. It alternated between learning from
real experience and learning from experience the model made up.

Ha and Schmidhuber's *World Models* (2018) made the label popular. Their system
had three pieces: an encoder <Gloss>a network that squeezes a video frame down
to a short list of numbers</Gloss>, a dynamics model that predicted where those
numbers go next, and a small controller trained almost entirely inside the
model's own imagined rollouts.

PlaNet (2018) learned those dynamics straight from pixels and planned in latent
space <Gloss>latent just means the model's own compressed description:
a few hundred numbers instead of a million pixels</Gloss>. Dreamer (2019) used
the same machinery to learn behaviour from imagined trajectories.

Then the word travelled. JEPA stopped predicting what the next frame looks like
and started predicting a summary of it. Genie, Cosmos and Marble pushed the
other way, toward worlds you can see and walk through. Four traditions, each
holding a different piece of one problem. They only noticed they were
neighbours when the outputs started to resemble each other.

## The five senses

These are contracts, not cages. They describe what a system promises, and
modern work crosses between them routinely.

<Figure
  n="1.3"
  caption={<>Four are systems you can run, ordered by how abstract the predicted object is. The fifth is not a system, which is why it sits off the axis.</>}
>
  <SenseMap />
</Figure>

**The Renderer** predicts observations, usually pixels. GameNGen generates DOOM
frames conditioned on previous frames and inputs. Genie 3 generates interactive
video at 720p and 24 frames per second, stays coherent for several minutes, and
accepts natural-language interventions mid-session: change the weather, add a
flock of birds. Persistence exists, but it is learned through generation
rather than guaranteed by stored geometry.

**The Simulator** predicts queryable structure. Marble takes text, an image or
a rough 3-D layout and returns two representations at once. Gaussian splats
<Gloss>millions of coloured blobs, cheap to render, nothing to bump
into</Gloss> carry the appearance. Triangle meshes carry the structure,
collider meshes included <Gloss>the invisible geometry a physics engine
actually collides against</Gloss>. World Labs shipped it to limited beta in
November 2025 and general availability in February 2026. NVIDIA open-sourced Cosmos, which
generates physically-aware video specifically as robot and autonomous-vehicle
training data.

Cosmos is the useful boundary case. NVIDIA describes Cosmos Predict as
generative video, while Omniverse supplies the explicit 3-D simulation. File
the whole platform under "simulator" and you hide the distinction worth
learning.

**The Controller** predicts compact state and reward under actions. PlaNet
plans online in latent space; Dreamer learns behaviour from trajectories
imagined through those dynamics. What matters here is not how real it looks. It is whether you can search inside
it. Try a hundred possible action sequences, score them, and keep the best
one.

**The Representation** predicts embeddings <Gloss>a list of numbers standing in
for an image or a clip, arranged so similar things sit close together</Gloss>
and then throws the prediction away. I-JEPA hides part of an image and predicts
the embedding of the missing piece, rather than redrawing the pixels. V-JEPA 2 pre-trains on more than a million hours of video with no actions
involved. A second stage post-trains an action-conditioned variant for
model-predictive control <Gloss>plan a few steps, execute one,
re-plan</Gloss>. Meta reports it driving a Franka arm in a lab it never saw
during training, and planning around 30&times; faster than Cosmos. Treat that
number as a comparison between different contracts rather than a ranking.

**The Implicit Model** is a different kind of claim. Othello-GPT was trained
only to predict legal Othello moves and never shown a board. Li et al. (2022)
found board state represented internally, recoverable by a probe
<Gloss>a small classifier trained to read one specific fact out of a network's
activations</Gloss>. Intervening on that representation changed the moves the
model went on to make. Nanda et al. (2023) later found a closely related
linear representation. Nobody handed the model a callable function named
`world_model`. The phrase describes evidence about what arose inside another
system.

## The test

There is one question people reach for first.

Turn around. Walk away. Turn back. Is it the same room?

It sorts the five tabs in about a minute. It is also wrong.

DeepMind reports Genie 3 recalling previously seen detail across multi-minute
interactions. A renderer can hold a room with no geometry to hold it in.

Keep it anyway. It was asking a better question than the one it was hired for.

When the room leaves your field of view, it leaves your observation. It does
not leave the world.

<Quote
  who="World Labs"
  context="on the distinction underneath the taxonomy"
  where="A Functional Taxonomy of World Models, June 2026"
  href="https://www.worldlabs.ai/blog/taxonomy-of-world-models"
>
  State is the underlying reality of the world; complete in principle, but
  never directly visible to any agent inside it. Observations are an agent's
  partial view of that reality.
</Quote>

The gap between state and observation has a name: partial observability
<Gloss>you only ever see part of what is going on, so you have to work out the
rest</Gloss>. The rest of this course lives inside it. The true state might include the chair
behind you, the velocity of a ball already rolling, whether a cupboard is open.
Your camera holds only what is visible now.

The idea is older than any of the systems above.

<Quote
  who="Kenneth Craik"
  context="on internal models, forty years before anyone trained one"
  where="The Nature of Explanation, 1943"
>
  If the organism carries a &lsquo;small-scale model&rsquo; of external reality
  and of its own possible actions within its head, it is able to try out
  various alternatives, conclude which is the best of them, react to future
  situations before they arise&hellip; and in every way to react in a much
  fuller, safer, and more competent manner to the emergencies which face it.
</Quote>

## The loop

One object sits underneath all five senses.

An environment has a state. An agent receives observations revealing part of
it. From observations and memory it builds an internal state. A model predicts
how things could evolve. The agent acts. The world changes. A new observation
arrives.

>
  <AgentLoop />
</Figure>

Three questions follow directly, and each gets a chapter.

**Where is the state?** Throw a ball around a corner and a single coordinate is
a weak answer. PlaNet deliberately combines deterministic and stochastic latent
components because uncertainty is not model failure; unearned certainty is.

**Do actions change the prediction?** *What happens next* and *what happens if I
push this* are different questions. Only the second one lets you compare
options before choosing. A model that answers it is called action-conditioned
<Gloss>it is told which action was taken, so it can answer what-if instead of
only what-next</Gloss>, and that is what control needs.

**How far can it roll forward?** Planning feeds predictions back into
themselves:

$$
p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t) \;\Longrightarrow\; p(s_{t+1:t+H} \mid s_t, a_{t:t+H-1})
$$

$H$ is the horizon: how many steps ahead you are asking about. Errors pile up
along it, because each imagined state becomes the input to the next prediction.
Get step one slightly wrong and step twenty can be somewhere else entirely.

Dreamer exists to learn behaviour across many imagined steps. DeepMind names
long-horizon consistency as a central open problem for generated worlds. Same
enemy, opposite ends of the field.

## What to ask next time

Given anything labelled a world model:

- **What is predicted?** Pixels, explicit structure, compact latent state,
  embeddings, or nothing because the claim is about internals.
- **Where is the state?** Observed, inferred from history, represented
  geometrically, or asserted to exist inside activations.
- **Can actions change it?** Prediction and prediction-under-intervention are
  different.
- **What survives leaving frame?** Explicit, learned, approximate, or outside
  the contract.
- **Does it keep more than one future?** Or collapse early to one.
- **How many steps before it breaks?** A good one-step predictor can be useless
  by step twenty.
- **What would falsify it?** An implausible frame, inconsistent geometry, wrong
  latent dynamics and an uninterpretable representation are four different
  failures.

Where a claim holds in only one sense, this course says which.

Now go back to the meeting. Somebody says world model, and the useful move is
no longer to nod. It is to ask which of the five they mean, and if they cannot
say, that is information too.
