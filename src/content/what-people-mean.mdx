A clip goes past on your timeline. Somebody is walking through a landscape with
the arrow keys, and the caption says it was generated, not built. It looks like
a video game nobody made. The replies call it a world model, so you go and look
the phrase up.

The mess has a history, and the history is tidier than the tabs. Between about
2018 and 2024, four fields reached the same two words from four directions.
They were computer vision, reinforcement learning, robotics and
interpretability, the study of what goes on inside a trained model. Each meant
something real, and none of them had to check what the others meant.

By 2026 the labs had started publishing dictionaries. Five definitions are in
current use, each out of its own tradition and its own year. There is a
four-second test that ought to tell them apart, and it does not.

## One equation, sixty years

The term started somewhere specific. Everything after it either grew from that
start or pushed against it. The start was control theory, the engineering maths
of keeping a machine on course: an autopilot, a thermostat, a rocket.

Later it moved into reinforcement learning <Gloss>training an agent by letting
it act, watching what happens, and rewarding what worked</Gloss>. There, a
world model is a learned transition function. That is a rule for what comes
next, one the agent picks up from experience.

A state and an action go in, and the next state comes out. State here means a
description of how things stand at one moment. Written down it looks like this.

<Figure
  n="1.3"
  caption={<>Every system below is an argument about what belongs in place of <em>s</em>. It could be pixels, 3-D geometry, a short list of numbers, or an embedding. That choice is the main axis the five definitions separate along.</>}
>
  <TransitionEquation />
</Figure>

The oldest ancestor is the Kalman filter, from 1960. Rudolf Kalman, a
Hungarian-born engineer, set it out in *A New Approach to Linear Filtering and
Prediction Problems*. A filter here is a piece of maths that cleans up noisy
readings. Feed it a radar's blips on a plane, and it keeps a running best guess
of where the plane is and how fast it is going.

Within a decade it was flying in Apollo's guidance computer. It is still the
maths inside a GPS receiver. That is one half of what a world model does.
There is a hidden state you never see directly, and the job is to estimate it
from what you can see.

The trick inside it is the same one that runs in every GPS receiver, drone
and self-driving car. At each moment you have two
stories about where the plane is. One comes from physics: take the last
position and the last speed, and predict where it should be now. The other
comes from the radar, which hands you a blip.

Both stories are uncertain. Each is really a bell curve, a Gaussian, with the
most likely spot in the middle and the less likely ones fading out either side.
Multiply the two curves together and you get a third bell curve. It sits
between the two, and it is narrower than either of them. That is the new
belief.

<Figure
  n="1.4"
  caption={<>Two stories about where the plane is, drawn as bell curves on one axis. Slide how sure physics is and how noisy the radar is, and watch the new belief lean toward the sharper story while staying narrower than either. The gain is the number that says how far it leaned. Press Correct to make the new belief the next physics prediction, then see it widen again as the plane moves on.</>}
>
  <TwoStories />
</Figure>

How far the new belief leans from physics toward the radar is set by one
number between zero and one, which the field calls the Kalman gain. A noisy radar pulls
the gain toward zero, so the filter sticks with physics. A sharp radar pulls
it toward one, so the filter trusts the reading.

Then it repeats. Predict, correct, predict, correct. Every new blip, however
wrong on its own, tightens what the filter knows. The estimate hugs the truth
even though every reading it ever got was off, and it does that one tick at a
time without ever waiting for the future.

<Figure
  n="1.5"
  caption={<>A plane flies on a smooth path. A radar reports its position every couple of seconds, with noise on top. A Kalman filter keeps a running guess of where the plane is and how fast it is going. Scrub the time or press play, and watch the band around the estimate tighten as blips arrive. Turn the radar noise up, and the estimate still sits closer to the plane than the blips do. Drop the blips for a stretch, and it coasts on its own dynamics while the band opens. The gain readout is the lean: near zero it trusts physics, near one it trusts the radar. The greyed control is the point: nothing here can ask what happens if the plane turns. Distances are illustrative.</>}
>
  <KalmanTracker />
</Figure>

It never asks the other half, what happens if I act. The dynamics in a Kalman
filter, the rules for how the state moves, are written in by the engineer
rather than learned. It never compares two actions either. That is why it is an
ancestor rather than a member.

The other half took thirty years and a different field. By 1990 neural
networks, programs that learn from examples rather than from rules someone
typed in, were back in fashion. Three groups reached for the same design in the
same year.

Jürgen Schmidhuber, then a young researcher in Munich, built a system in two
parts. His paper was *Recurrent world models for planning and curiosity*. One
network learned to predict what the world would do next. A second chose
actions, and the first told it what each choice would lead to.

<Figure
  n="1.6"
  caption={<>The 1990 design, in miniature. Point at an arrow, and the world model draws where it thinks you would land. Press it, and the world answers. The model knows the walls but not the patches: ice carries you on, drift and lift push you off your row. Switch on "Learn from mistakes" and press "Run a lap" three times. The surprises fall lap by lap, because the chooser now plans inside a model that knows the route. Then press "Forget", switch learning off, and run three more: every lap is as surprised as the first.</>}
>
  <TwoNetworks />
</Figure>

He called the first one the world model, and that is the phrase's birth
certificate. Schmidhuber, later co-inventor of the LSTM memory cell, has spent
a career insisting the field forgets who had its ideas first. On this one the
record is plainly his.

In the same period Richard Sutton built Dyna. It was a learning agent that
practised partly on real experience and partly on experience its own model made
up. His paper was *Dyna: an Integrated Architecture for Learning, Planning and
Reacting*.

Sutton and Andrew Barto went on to write the textbook every reinforcement
learning student still reads. In 2024 the two shared the Turing Award.

<Figure
  n="1.7"
  caption={<>Three systems from 1990 and 1991, each drawn as a loop. Click a card to enlarge it and read what its model does and what uses it. Then turn on the shared shape: the world and the arrows fade, and the same two boxes light up on every card.</>}
>
  <ThreePapers1990 />

Sebastian Thrun, Knut Möller and Alexander Linden made the third, *Planning
with an Adaptive World Model*. All three had the shape of the modern idea. A
learned model says what happens next, and something else uses it to decide.

The label did not stick. The networks of 1990 were small, and their worlds
were mazes and a few dozen numbers. Nobody could learn a useful model of
anything with a camera in it.

For most of the next twenty-five years the model-based line, agents that carry
a model and use it, stayed a minority interest. When deep learning reached
reinforcement learning, the headline result was DeepMind's DQN in 2013. It
learned Atari games from pixels with no model of the game at all. Model-free
had the momentum, and the two-part design of 1990 looked like a relic.

David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber's *World Models* (2018) is the paper that made
the label popular. I read it as a reply to that momentum. Ha was then at Google
Brain. The paper showed the 1990 design working on a car-racing game and a
level of the video game Doom, in three pieces.

The first is an encoder <Gloss>a network that squeezes a video frame down to a
short list of numbers</Gloss>, thirty-two numbers here. The second is a
dynamics model, which predicted where those numbers go next. The third is a
small controller, the piece that picks actions.

The controller was trained almost entirely inside the model's own imagined
rollouts, futures the model played out for itself. Then it moved back into the
real game, where it still worked.

  n="1.9"
  caption={<>Ha and Schmidhuber's three pieces as a loop. Select a box to see what goes in and what comes out, then press Step and watch the thirty-two numbers change. The name can mislead anyone who goes on to the papers. Ha and Schmidhuber call
their third module the controller, the small policy that picks actions. The
world model is the module beside it, the one the controller plans inside.
Calling the whole thing "the controller" would name it after the one piece
that is plainly not a world model.

PlaNet came the same year, from Danijar Hafner and colleagues, with Ha among
the authors. The paper was *Learning Latent Dynamics for Planning from Pixels*.
It learned those dynamics straight from pixels. Then it planned in latent
space <Gloss>latent just means the model's own compressed description: a few
hundred numbers instead of a million pixels</Gloss>.

At every step it tried many action sequences inside the model and took the
best first move. Dreamer (2019) came from the same group, in a paper called
*Dream to Control*. Instead of searching for a plan at every step, it learned
its behaviour from futures imagined inside the model.

  n="1.10"

>
  <PlanOrLearn />

Its third version reached *Nature* in 2025 with one set of settings across more
than 150 tasks. That is as close to a dynasty as this field gets. By the end of
2019, though, the reinforcement learning reading of the word was settled.

Then the word travelled, in two directions at once. In 2022 Yann LeCun
published *A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence*. He is Meta's chief
AI scientist and a Turing Award winner. He argued that predicting every pixel
of the next frame is the wrong job, because most pixels are detail nobody can
predict.

JEPA, the family of models that followed at Meta, predicts a summary of the
next frame instead. That lets it drop the decoder. A decoder is the part that
would have turned the prediction back into pixels.

The other direction went toward worlds you can see and walk through. In
February 2024 OpenAI published Sora with a report titled *Video generation
models as world simulators*. The same month Genie arrived from Jake Bruce and
colleagues at DeepMind. It learned its own set of actions from unlabelled
internet video of games, then let you play what it drew.

Cosmos from NVIDIA and Marble from World Labs followed in 2025. Meanwhile the
interpretability people had borrowed the word for something with no demo at
all. Their claim was that a model trained for one job had grown a model of the
world inside itself.

By June 2026 World Labs had published *A Functional Taxonomy of World Models*,
a sorting system for the mess. The dictionaries had begun. Four traditions each
held a different piece of one problem. From outside the term looks confused.

The four only noticed they were neighbours when their outputs started to look
alike. By then each had been using the word for years.

## The five definitions

Treat these categories as contracts rather than species. Each describes what a
system promises to hand you. Modern work crosses between them all the time, as
NVIDIA's Cosmos and Meta's V-JEPA 2 are about to show.

<Figure
  n="1.13"
  caption={<>Four are systems you can run. They are ordered by how abstract the predicted object is. The fifth is a claim about another system rather than a system itself, so it sits off the axis.</>}
>
  <DefinitionMap />
</Figure>

**The Renderer** predicts observations, usually pixels. GameNGen, from Dani
Valevski and colleagues at Google (2024), draws frames of the video game DOOM.
Each frame comes from the frames before it and the player's inputs. It runs at
about twenty frames a second on a single TPU chip, which is fast enough to
play.

Genie 3, from DeepMind, is reported to generate interactive video at 720p and
24 frames per second. It is said to stay coherent for several minutes, with the
scene hanging together as you move. It also takes plain-language instructions
mid-session: change the weather, add a flock of birds.

Persistence exists, meaning things stay where you left them. It is learned
through generation, though, rather than guaranteed by stored geometry.

**The Simulator** predicts structure you can query, meaning 3-D shapes you can
ask questions of. Marble comes from World Labs, a start-up co-founded by the
Stanford researcher behind ImageNet. That is the picture dataset that set off
the deep learning boom. Marble takes text, an image or a rough 3-D layout, and
hands back two things at once.

Gaussian splats <Gloss>millions of coloured blobs, cheap to render, nothing to
bump into</Gloss> carry the look. Triangle meshes carry the structure. A mesh
is a surface built from many small triangles. That is ordinary 3-D geometry,
collider meshes included <Gloss>the invisible shapes a physics engine bumps
into</Gloss>.

World Labs shipped Marble to a limited beta in November 2025 and opened it to
everyone in February 2026. NVIDIA open-sourced Cosmos in January 2025. It
generates physically-aware video, built as training data for robots and
self-driving cars. It is also the case that keeps the map honest.

from yourself.

A platform is not a category. One product can expose a pixel predictor, a 3-D
simulator and an action-conditioned model all at once. Action-conditioned means
it takes your actions as input. Ask which one you are being sold, and which
interface you are about to build against.

**The Dynamics Model** predicts compact state under actions, often with the
reward. Compact means a short list of numbers rather than a picture. It is the
oldest of the four runnable categories. It is the one the line from Schmidhuber
to Dreamer was building toward.

MuZero, from Julian Schrittwieser and colleagues at DeepMind, is the proof. Its
model predicted only reward, value and likely moves, never the board. With that
it matched AlphaZero, its famous predecessor, at Go, chess and shogi. It was
never told the rules.

PlaNet plans inside it, searching afresh at every step. Dreamer learns
behaviour from futures imagined through it. What matters here is whether you
can search inside it. How real it looks does not come into it.

**The Representation** predicts embeddings <Gloss>a list of numbers standing in
for an image or a clip, with similar things close together</Gloss>. Then it
throws the prediction away. I-JEPA (2023), the image version from Mahmoud
Assran and colleagues, hides part of an image and predicts the embedding of the
missing piece. It does not redraw the pixels.

V-JEPA 2 (2025), the video version, pre-trains on more than a million hours of
video with no actions involved. A second stage then trains an
action-conditioned variant on top. That one is built for model-predictive
control <Gloss>plan a few steps, take one, plan again</Gloss>. Meta reports it
driving a Franka robot arm in a lab it never saw during training.

Meta also reports it planning around 30&times; faster than Cosmos. That number
compares two different contracts rather than ranking them. Anyone quoting it as
a ranking has not read the contracts.

That second stage takes actions, rolls forward and gets searched over. That is
the Dynamics Model's contract, reached from the other side. Call it migration:
a system starts in the category it was trained for and grows the machinery of
another on top. The categories describe a training target, not a fixed
identity.

**The Implicit Model** is a different kind of claim. It is the least discussed
of the five, because it has no demo.

Othello-GPT is a small language model trained only to predict legal moves in
Othello, a board game played with two-sided discs. It was never shown a board.
Kenneth Li and colleagues (2022), who trained it, reported in *Emergent World
Representations* that the board was there inside it anyway.

A probe <Gloss>a small classifier trained to read one specific fact out of a
network's activations</Gloss> could read it out. When they changed that inner
board by hand, the model's next moves changed too.

Neel Nanda and colleagues (2023) followed up, in a paper called *Othello-GPT
has a linear emergent world representation*. The board was there in a form
plain enough for the simplest probe to read. You only had to ask for "mine" and
"theirs" instead of black and white.

Nobody handed the model a function called `world_model`. The phrase here is a
claim about what grew inside another system. It is a finding, not an interface.

## The test that was supposed to settle it

One test ought to sort all five out, and it takes about four seconds. Turn
around and walk away, then turn back. Is it the same room? Something that holds
a world inside it keeps the furniture where you left it, and something that is
only drawing pictures cannot. Call it the turn-around test.

<Figure
  n="1.19"
  caption={<>The turn-around test, made playable. With the switch off, press Turn away and back and read where the vermilion post comes back. Then switch the world on and run it again. The panel on the right shows what is stored in each case.</>}
>
  <GeneratedLandscape mode="test" />
</Figure>

It does not work, and the figure above shows why. Leave the switch off and
press "Turn away and back". The post has drifted, because nothing behind the
picture is holding it there.

Now turn *hold the world* on and do it again. The post stays, because a
stored coordinate is being read back.

Those are the two ways to pass the test. Store the room and look it up when the
viewer turns back. Or get very good at drawing a room that matches the one you
drew a moment ago. From outside they look the same, and only the switch tells
them apart.

A large model trained on a lot of video learns the second way. Nobody gave it a
room to keep. It got good at continuing what it had already started, and
continuing consistently is part of that. That is why Genie 3's persistence is
real, and why the turn-around test was never going to catch it.

The turn-around test cannot sort them, then, but it was reaching for the right
distinction. When you turn away from the chair, the chair does not stop
existing. It stops being *visible*. Those are two different things, and nearly
everything hard about this subject lives in the gap between them.

What is there, whether or not you can see it, is called the **state**. The part
of it you can see right now is called the **observation**.

<Quote
  who="World Labs"
  context="on the distinction underneath the taxonomy"
  where="A Functional Taxonomy of World Models, June 2026"
  href="https://www.worldlabs.ai/blog/taxonomy-of-world-models"

  State is the underlying reality of the world; complete in principle, but
  never directly visible to any agent inside it. Observations are an agent's
  partial view of that reality.
</Quote>

You never get the state, only observations, and you work backwards from them.
Think of the chair behind you, a ball still rolling out of shot, or whether the
cupboard is open. All of it is real and none of it is visible.

Working out the rest from the part you can see is called **partial
observability**.

None of this is new, and the date is the part I like. A Cambridge psychologist,
Kenneth Craik, described the fix in 1943, in a book called *The Nature of
Explanation*.

He was twenty-nine when it came out. Two years later he was dead, knocked off
his bicycle on a Cambridge street. That was seventeen years before Kalman, and
long before there was anything to build it out of.

<Quote
  who="Kenneth Craik"
  context="on internal models, forty years before anyone trained one"
  where="The Nature of Explanation, 1943"
>
  If the organism carries a &lsquo;small-scale model&rsquo; of external reality
  and of its own possible actions within its head, it is able to try out
  various alternatives, conclude which is the best of them, react to future
  situations before they arise&hellip; and in every way to react in a much
  fuller, safer, and more competent manner to the emergencies which face it.
</Quote>

## What they were all fighting over

One object sits underneath all five definitions. World Labs derive their own
taxonomy from the same object, and Sutton's Dyna was running round it in 1990.

An environment has a state. An agent receives observations that reveal part of
it, and from observations and memory it builds an internal state. A model
predicts how things could change. The agent acts, the world changes, and a new
observation arrives.

<Figure
  n="1.23"
  caption={<>The agent-environment loop, with each definition shown as a specialist on one arc. Systems that look incompatible share a name because each owns a piece of the same loop.</>}
>
  <AgentLoop />
</Figure>

Draw the loop that way and three problems fall out of it. They are the same
three problems the field has been arguing about since Kalman wrote down a
filter in 1960. Each gets its own chapter later. The short versions are these.

**How sure should it be?** Throw a ball behind a wall and ask where it is now.
A single exact position is the wrong shape of answer, because you cannot know.
It might have bounced off something, or stopped against a kerb, or still be
rolling.

A model that hands back one confident coordinate has thrown away the fact that
it was guessing. PlaNet carries two things at once instead. One part is
deterministic <Gloss>computed the same way every time, so it always follows
from the last state</Gloss>. It holds whatever reliably follows from what came
before.

The other part is stochastic <Gloss>sampled rather than computed, so the same
input can produce several different futures</Gloss>. It holds whatever could
still go either way. The split is the model admitting what it does not know.

<Figure
  n="1.24"
  caption={<>Drag time forward and watch the ball go behind the wall. The faint dot is the answer a deterministic model gives: one coordinate, moved on at the known speed. Switch on the stochastic part and the model also carries a spread that widens the longer the ball is hidden. Keep going past the point where the dot comes out, and ask which answer was honest.</>}
>
  <BallBehindWall />
</Figure>

**Do actions change the prediction?** *What happens next* and *what happens if
I push this* are different questions. Only the second one lets you compare
options before choosing.

A model that answers it is called action-conditioned <Gloss>it is told which
action was taken, so it can answer what-if instead of only what-next</Gloss>.
That is what control needs. Kalman's filter never had it.

<Figure
  n="1.25"
  caption={<>One start, three futures, chosen by which key you hold. Turn the action input off and the three rows fold into one: without an action there is only what probably happens next, not what happens if you do this.</>}
>
  <ActionConditioned />
</Figure>

**How far can it roll forward?** One prediction is rarely enough. To plan, a
model has to feed its own answer back in and predict again, and again, on top
of what it just made up. The number of steps you ask for is called the
**horizon**.

Errors pile up along that stretch. Nothing dramatic happens at any single step,
which is what makes the problem hard to notice. The model is only ever slightly
wrong. Get step one slightly wrong and step twenty can be somewhere else
entirely.

<Figure
  n="1.26"
  caption={<>The same model asked for a stretch of future instead of a single step. Two things grow and one does not, and that is the difficulty.</>}
>

Dreamer exists to learn behaviour across many imagined steps. DeepMind names
long-horizon coherence as the big open problem for its generated worlds. That
means a scene that stays true to itself over many steps.

Dreamer is a latent dynamics model from 2019, and Genie 3 is a pixel renderer
from 2025. They sit at the two ends of the map, fighting the same problem from
opposite sides.

Sixty years on, the open problem is drift, the thing Kalman's filter was built
to correct. The difference is that an imagined rollout has no fresh readings to
correct against.

Now go back to the clip. It is a Renderer, and you can say why. It predicts
observations. It holds the room because it learned to, and nothing in it
promises geometry underneath.

The next one that goes past your timeline will be called a world model too. The
question to ask is which of the five it is, and whether the person posting it
could tell you.
