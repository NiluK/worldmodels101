A clip goes past on your timeline. Somebody is walking through a landscape with
the arrow keys, and the caption says it was generated, not built. It looks like
a video game nobody made. The replies call it a world model, so you go and look
the phrase up.

Ten minutes later there are five tabs open, and they describe five different
machines. One generates video you steer with the arrow keys. One exports
geometry into Blender, the 3-D modelling program. One is a paper about
predicting embeddings, and it never shows you a video at all.

The mess has a history, and the history is tidier than the tabs. Between about
2018 and 2024, four fields reached the same two words from four directions:
computer vision, reinforcement learning, robotics and interpretability. Each
meant something real, and none of them had to check what the others meant.

By 2026 the labs had started publishing dictionaries. Five definitions are in
current use, each out of its own tradition and its own year. There is a
four-second test that ought to tell them apart, and it does not.

## One equation, sixty years

The term started somewhere specific. Everything after it either grew from that
start or pushed against it. The start was control theory, the engineering maths
of keeping a machine on course (an autopilot, a thermostat, a rocket). Later it
was reinforcement learning <Gloss>training an agent by letting it act, watching
what happens, and rewarding what worked</Gloss>.

There, a world model is a learned transition function. A state and an action go
in, and the next state comes out. State here means a description of how things
stand at one moment. Written down it looks like this.

The oldest ancestor is the Kalman filter, from 1960. Rudolf Kalman, a
Hungarian-born engineer, set it out in *A New Approach to Linear Filtering and
Prediction Problems*. It takes noisy readings, say a radar's blips on a plane,
and keeps a running best guess of where the plane is and how fast it is going.
Within a decade it was flying in Apollo's guidance computer, and it is still
the maths inside a GPS receiver.

That is one half of what a world model does: there is a hidden state you never
see directly, and the job is to estimate it from what you can see. It never
asks the other half, what happens if I act. The dynamics in a Kalman filter are
supplied by the engineer, not learned, and it never compares two actions. That
is why it is an ancestor rather than a member.

The other half took thirty years and a different field. By 1990 neural networks
were back in fashion, and three groups reached for the same design in the same
year. Jürgen Schmidhuber, then a young researcher in Munich, built a system in
two parts. His paper was *Recurrent world models for planning and curiosity*.

One network learned to predict what the world would do next. A second chose
actions, and the first told it what each choice would lead to. He called the
first one the world model, and that is the phrase's birth certificate.

Schmidhuber, later co-inventor of the LSTM memory cell, has spent a career
insisting the field forgets who had its ideas first. On this one the record is
plainly his.

In the same period Richard Sutton built Dyna. It was a learning agent that
practised partly on real experience and partly on experience its own model made
up. His paper was *Dyna: an Integrated Architecture for Learning, Planning and
Reacting*. Sutton would go on, with Barto, to write the textbook every
reinforcement learning student still reads, and to share the 2024 Turing Award.

Sebastian Thrun, Möller and Linden made the third, *Planning with an Adaptive
World Model*. All three had the shape of the modern idea: a learned model of
what happens next, and something else that uses it to decide. The label did not
stick, because the networks of 1990 were small and their worlds were mazes and
a few dozen numbers. Nobody could learn a useful model of anything with a
camera in it.

For most of the next twenty-five years the model-based line stayed a minority
interest. The headline result when deep learning reached reinforcement learning
was DeepMind's DQN in 2013, which learned Atari games from pixels with no model
of the game at all. Model-free had the momentum, and the two-part design of
1990 looked like a relic.

David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber's *World Models* (2018) is the paper that made
the label popular, and I read it as a reply to that momentum. Ha was then at
Google Brain. The paper showed the 1990 design working on a car-racing game and
a level of the video game Doom, in three pieces.

The first is an encoder <Gloss>a network that squeezes a video frame down to a
short list of numbers</Gloss>, thirty-two numbers here. The second is a
dynamics model that predicted where those numbers go next. The third is a small
controller, the piece that picks actions. It was trained almost entirely inside
the model's own imagined rollouts, futures the model played out for itself,
then moved back into the real game, where it still worked.

PlaNet came the same year, from Danijar Hafner and colleagues, with Ha among
the authors. The paper was *Learning Latent Dynamics for Planning from Pixels*.
It learned those dynamics straight from pixels, then planned in latent space
<Gloss>latent just means the model's own compressed description: a few hundred
numbers instead of a million pixels</Gloss>. At every step it tried many action
sequences inside the model and took the best first move.

Dreamer (2019) came from the same group, in a paper called *Dream to Control*.
Instead of searching for a plan at every step, it learned its behaviour from
futures imagined inside the model. Its third version reached *Nature* in 2025
with one set of settings across more than 150 tasks, which is as close to a
dynasty as this field gets. By the end of 2019, though, the reinforcement
learning reading of the word was settled.

Then the word travelled, in two directions at once. In 2022 Yann LeCun, Meta's
chief AI scientist and a Turing Award winner, published *A Path Towards
Autonomous Machine Intelligence*. It argued that predicting every pixel of the
next frame is the wrong job, because most pixels are detail nobody can predict.

JEPA, the family of models that followed at Meta, predicts a summary of the
next frame instead. That lets it drop the decoder, the part that would have
turned the prediction back into pixels.

The other direction went toward worlds you can see and walk through. In
February 2024 OpenAI published Sora with a report titled *Video generation
models as world simulators*. The same month Genie arrived from Jake Bruce and
colleagues at DeepMind. It learned its own set of actions from unlabelled
internet video of games and let you play what it drew.

Cosmos from NVIDIA and Marble from World Labs followed in 2025. Meanwhile the
interpretability people had borrowed the word for something with no demo at
all. Their claim was that a model trained for one job had grown a model of the
world inside itself. By June 2026 World Labs had published *A Functional
Taxonomy of World Models* to sort the mess, and the dictionaries had begun.

Four traditions each held a different piece of one problem. From outside the
term looks confused. That is because the four only noticed they were neighbours
when their outputs started to look alike, and by then each had been using the
word for years.

## The five definitions

Treat these categories as contracts rather than species. Each describes what a
system promises to hand you. Modern work crosses between them all the time, as
NVIDIA's Cosmos and Meta's V-JEPA 2 are about to show.

**The Renderer** predicts observations, usually pixels. GameNGen, from Dani
Valevski and colleagues at Google (2024), draws frames of the video game DOOM,
each one from the frames before it and the player's inputs. It runs at about
twenty frames a second on a single TPU chip, which is fast enough to play.

Genie 3, from DeepMind, is reported to generate interactive video at 720p and
24 frames per second. It is said to stay coherent for several minutes. It also
takes plain-language instructions mid-session: change the weather, add a flock
of birds. Persistence exists, but it is learned through generation, not
guaranteed by stored geometry.

**The Simulator** predicts structure you can query. Marble comes from World
Labs, a start-up co-founded by the Stanford researcher behind ImageNet, the
dataset that set off the deep learning boom. It takes text, an image or a rough
3-D layout and hands back two things at once.

Gaussian splats <Gloss>millions of coloured blobs, cheap to render, nothing to
bump into</Gloss> carry the look. Triangle meshes carry the structure. That is
ordinary 3-D geometry, collider meshes included <Gloss>the invisible shapes a
physics engine bumps into</Gloss>. World Labs shipped it to a limited beta in
November 2025 and opened it to everyone in February 2026.

NVIDIA open-sourced Cosmos in January 2025. It generates physically-aware
video, built as training data for robots and self-driving cars. It is also the
case that keeps the map honest.

NVIDIA itself calls Cosmos Predict, the video-making part, generative video.
The real 3-D simulator is Omniverse, which is a different NVIDIA product. Put
the whole thing under "simulator" and you have hidden that split from yourself.

A platform is not a category. One product can expose a pixel predictor, a 3-D
simulator and an action-conditioned model (one that takes your actions as
input) at the same time. Ask which one you are being sold, and which interface
you are about to build against.

**The Dynamics Model** predicts compact state under actions, often with the
reward. It is the oldest of the four runnable categories, the one the line from
Schmidhuber to Dreamer was building toward. PlaNet plans inside it, searching
afresh at every step. Dreamer learns behaviour from futures imagined through
it.

What matters here is whether you can search inside it, and how real it looks
does not come into it. MuZero, from Julian Schrittwieser and colleagues at
DeepMind, is the proof. Its model predicted only reward, value and likely
moves, never the board. With that it matched AlphaZero, its famous predecessor,
at Go, chess and shogi without being told the rules.

The name can mislead anyone who goes on to the papers. Ha and Schmidhuber call
their third module the controller, the small policy that picks actions. The
world model is the module beside it, the one the controller plans inside.
Naming this category "the Controller" would name it after the one piece that is
plainly not a world model.

**The Representation** predicts embeddings <Gloss>a list of numbers standing in
for an image or a clip, with similar things close together</Gloss>. Then it
throws the prediction away. I-JEPA (2023), the image version from Mahmoud
Assran and colleagues, hides part of an image and predicts the embedding of the
missing piece. It does not redraw the pixels.

V-JEPA 2 (2025), the video version, pre-trains on more than a million hours of
video with no actions involved. Then a second stage post-trains an
action-conditioned variant for model-predictive control <Gloss>plan a few
steps, take one, plan again</Gloss>. Meta reports it driving a Franka robot arm
in a lab it never saw during training.

Meta also reports it planning around 30&times; faster than Cosmos. That number
compares two different contracts rather than ranking them. Anyone quoting it as
a ranking has not read the contracts.

That second stage is action-conditioned, rolled forward and searched over. That
is the Dynamics Model's contract, reached from the other direction. Call it
migration: a system starts in the category it was trained for and grows the
machinery of another on top. The categories describe a training target, not a
permanent identity.

**The Implicit Model** is a different kind of claim, and the least discussed of
the five, because it has no demo.

Othello-GPT is a small language model trained only to predict legal moves in
Othello, a board game played with two-sided discs. It was never shown a board.
Kenneth Li and colleagues (2022), who trained it, reported in *Emergent World
Representations* that the board was represented inside it. A probe <Gloss>a
small classifier trained to read one specific fact out of a network's
activations</Gloss> could read it out.

When they intervened on that representation, the moves the model went on to
make changed with it. Neel Nanda and colleagues (2023) followed up with a paper
called *Othello-GPT has a linear emergent world representation*. The board was
there in a form plain enough for the simplest probe to read, once you asked for
"mine" and "theirs" instead of black and white.

Nobody handed the model a function called `world_model`. The phrase here is a
claim about what grew inside another system. It is a finding, not an interface.

## The test that was supposed to settle it

One test ought to sort all five out, and it takes about four seconds. Turn
around and walk away, then turn back. Is it the same room?

Something that holds a world inside it will keep the furniture where you left
it, and something that is only drawing pictures cannot. Call it the turn-around
test.

It does not work, and Figure 1.1 shows why. Go back to it and leave the switch
alone. Walk away from the marker and come back. It has drifted, because nothing
behind the picture is holding it there.

Now turn *hold the world* on and do it again. The marker stays, because a
stored coordinate is being read back. Those are the two ways to pass the test:
store the room and look it up when the viewer turns back, or get very good at
drawing a room that matches the one you drew a moment ago. From outside they
look the same, and only the switch tells them apart.

A large model trained on a lot of video learns the second way. Nobody gave it a
room to keep. It got good at continuing what it had already started, and
continuing consistently is part of that. That is why Genie 3's persistence is
real, and why the turn-around test was never going to catch it.

<MarginNote>
DeepMind reports this of Genie 3: scenes holding together over several minutes,
and details coming back when you return to them. Take it as their report rather
than something anyone outside the lab can check.
</MarginNote>

The turn-around test cannot sort them, then, but it was reaching for the right
distinction. When you turn away from the chair, the chair does not stop
existing. It stops being *visible*. Those are two different things, and nearly
everything hard about this subject lives in the gap between them.

What is there, whether or not you can see it, is called the **state**. The part
of it you can see right now is called the **observation**.

<Quote
  who="World Labs"
  context="on the distinction underneath the taxonomy"
  where="A Functional Taxonomy of World Models, June 2026"
  href="https://www.worldlabs.ai/blog/taxonomy-of-world-models"
>
  State is the underlying reality of the world; complete in principle, but
  never directly visible to any agent inside it. Observations are an agent's
  partial view of that reality.
</Quote>

You never get the state, only observations, and you work backwards from them:
the chair behind you, a ball still rolling out of shot, whether the cupboard is
open. All of it is real and none of it is visible.

Working out the rest from the part you can see is called **partial
observability**.

None of this is new, and the date is the part I like. A Cambridge psychologist,
Kenneth Craik, described the fix in 1943, in a book called *The Nature of
Explanation*. He was twenty-nine when it came out and dead two years later,
knocked off his bicycle on a Cambridge street. That was seventeen years before
Kalman, and long before there was anything to build it out of.

<Quote
  who="Kenneth Craik"
  context="on internal models, forty years before anyone trained one"
  where="The Nature of Explanation, 1943"
>
  If the organism carries a &lsquo;small-scale model&rsquo; of external reality
  and of its own possible actions within its head, it is able to try out
  various alternatives, conclude which is the best of them, react to future
  situations before they arise&hellip; and in every way to react in a much
  fuller, safer, and more competent manner to the emergencies which face it.
</Quote>

Carry a small model of the world in your head, and you can test an action
before you take it. Every definition on the map is a different bet on what
Craik's small-scale model should contain.

## What they were all fighting over

One object sits underneath all five definitions. World Labs derive their own
taxonomy from the same object, and Sutton's Dyna was running round it in 1990.

An environment has a state. An agent receives observations that reveal part of
it, and from observations and memory it builds an internal state. A model
predicts how things could change. The agent acts, the world changes, and a new
observation arrives.

<Figure
  n="1.8"
  caption={<>The agent-environment loop, with each definition shown as a specialist on one arc. Systems that look incompatible share a name because each owns a piece of the same loop.</>}

Draw the loop that way and three problems fall out of it. They are the same
three problems the field has been arguing about since Kalman wrote down a
filter in 1960. Each gets its own chapter later. The short versions are these.

**How sure should it be?** Throw a ball behind a wall and ask where it is now.
A single exact position is the wrong shape of answer, because you cannot know.
It might have bounced off something, or stopped against a kerb, or still be
rolling. A model that hands back one confident coordinate has thrown away the
fact that it was guessing.

PlaNet carries two things at once instead. A deterministic part <Gloss>computed
the same way every time, so it always follows from the last state</Gloss> holds
whatever reliably follows from what came before. A stochastic part
<Gloss>sampled rather than computed, so the same input can produce several
different futures</Gloss> holds whatever could still go either way. The split
is the model admitting what it does not know.

**Do actions change the prediction?** *What happens next* and *what happens if
I push this* are different questions. Only the second one lets you compare
options before choosing. A model that answers it is called action-conditioned
<Gloss>it is told which action was taken, so it can answer what-if instead of
only what-next</Gloss>. That is what control needs, and what Kalman's filter
never had.

**How far can it roll forward?** One prediction is rarely enough. To plan, a
model has to feed its own answer back in and predict again, and again, on top
of what it just made up. The number of steps you ask for is called the
**horizon**.

<Figure
  n="1.9"
  caption={<>The same model asked for a stretch of future instead of a single step. Two things grow and one does not, and that is the difficulty.</>}
>

</Figure>

Errors pile up along that stretch. Nothing dramatic happens at any single step,
which is what makes the problem hard to notice. The model is only ever slightly
wrong. Get step one slightly wrong and step twenty can be somewhere else
entirely.

  n="1.10"
  caption={<>Both dots start in the same place under the same rules, except one is running dynamics that are a few percent off. Drag to about 8 and they are still together. Keep going. Nothing breaks at any step. The gap is what piles up.</>}
>

Dreamer exists to learn behaviour across many imagined steps. DeepMind names
long-horizon coherence as a central open problem for its generated worlds.
Dreamer, a latent dynamics model from 2019, and Genie 3, a pixel renderer from
2025, sit at the two ends of the map. They are fighting the same problem from
opposite sides.

Sixty years on, the open problem is drift, the thing Kalman's filter was built
to correct. The difference is that an imagined rollout has no fresh readings to
correct against.

Now go back to the clip. It is a Renderer, and you can say why. It predicts
observations. It holds the room because it learned to, and nothing in it
promises geometry underneath.

The next one that goes past your timeline will be called a world model too. The
question to ask is which of the five it is, and whether the person posting it
could tell you.
