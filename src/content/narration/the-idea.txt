[strong New Zealand accent] This chapter is about the oldest of the five definitions, and the one that gave the term its technical meaning.

Start with some arithmetic. A professional tennis serve arrives in roughly half a second. Getting a signal from the retina to the visual cortex takes somewhere around sixty to a hundred milliseconds. Getting a large muscle group to actually move … another hundred and fifty. Add the swing, and the returner is over budget before they have decided anything at all.

[curious] So they are not reacting to the ball. They can't be. They are reacting to a prediction of the ball, produced by something in the nervous system that took the toss, and the racket angle, and the first few frames of flight, and extrapolated.

That machinery is a world model under the Controller definition. A learned simulator you can roll forward. Give it a state and an action, and it hands you the next state.

[excited] Build one and three things fall out. Planning, because you can try an action inside the model before paying for it outside. Cheap learning, because reality is a slow and expensive teacher … and a model is a fast one. And counterfactuals, because you can ask what would have happened if you had done something else, which is a question the world itself flatly refuses to answer.

There's a demo further down with two cars braking before a wall. One brakes at a fixed distance. The other brakes when it predicts it is about to crash. At low speed you genuinely cannot tell them apart … and THAT is the interesting part, not the warm-up.

[sarcastic] Push the speed up, and only one of them is still doing its job.
